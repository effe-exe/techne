# The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick

Use this when: you are about to interview users or prospects about a problem or product idea; you are reading notes from interviews full of "love it" and "I'd definitely use that"; a feature request arrives and you need to decide what is behind it; you are planning when to stop learning and ask for a real commitment (a pilot, an intro, a pre-order).

Basis: the book was not read. These notes come from Fitzpatrick's own free material: the official site and teacher page (which list the chapter topics and suggested lessons), his long interview on The SaaS Podcast (full transcript, episode 206) and on The Knowledge podcast (full transcript), and his blog posts on robfitz.com about validation, bias, segmentation and "signal stacking". They cover the book's three rules, the bad-data taxonomy, the two-stage conversation, commitment and advancement, and segmentation well; they cover less of the "important questions"/big-three planning, finding conversations, and note-taking chapters.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Everyone lies to you about your idea, not out of malice but because they want to be kind, so the fix is not to avoid biased people (like your mom) but to ask questions so good that even your mom couldn't mislead you. Good questions are about the other person's life, past behavior and real money or time already spent, never about your idea or what they would do in the future. Compliments and hypotheticals are bad data; facts about their life and real commitments (time, reputation, money) are good data, and the meeting is only a success if it ends with one.

## Core concepts
- **The Mom Test (three rules, as the author explains them)** — talk about their life instead of your idea; ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future; talk less and listen more.
- **Good data vs bad data** — good: facts about what they already do and why, how much it costs, what they have tried; and committed currency (time, introductions, money). Bad: compliments ("love it"), opinions, hypotheticals and future promises ("I'd definitely buy that").
- **People can't predict their own behavior** — Fitzpatrick's gym example: describe a $60/month thing that makes you healthier and everyone wants it; name it "the gym" and they don't.
- **Pitching ends learning** — once you pitch, you expose your ego and invite compliments; you can still test usability, but you can no longer learn whether they care about the problem.
- **Two stages** — first a customer-only conversation (no product mentioned); then, with permission ("I'm working on something in this space, can I show you?"), a product conversation.
- **"Let me know when it launches"** — a compliment plus a stalling tactic; the polite way to end a meeting without deciding.
- **Commitment and advancement** — ask for something they would only give if serious: more time with a clear agenda, an intro to their boss, access to their technical or legal team, a pilot, a deposit or pre-order. The ask matches the stage.
- **Sharp questions are fine** — the issue isn't open vs closed questions but whether the question is about their life; a pointed question ("it must go wrong sometimes?") can open a long, useful story.
- **Feature requests are a metal detector beep** — a signal that something may be buried; dig for the motivation ("what would that let you do?") before acting.
- **Segmentation explains mixed signals** — if 2 of 10 care, you may have talked to the wrong mix; find what the two share (his example: universities that see themselves as experimenters) and talk to more of them.
- **Bias is never 0%, validation never 100%** — formal settings, groups and bad days add bias you can't remove; aim to reduce it, recognize which data is clean, and decide when to stop talking and start building.
- **Conversations are a hammer, not the whole toolbox** — they help most with market risk ("do people want this, will it fit their lives?") and in sales-led businesses; they help little with execution risk or new behaviors (3D printing, games), where prototypes and longer bets are needed.
- **Signal stacking** — combine conversations with time commitments, analytics and observed behavior after the product (e.g., a reader asking to share a draft with their team) for a fuller picture.
- **Share the raw data** — in a weekly team call, rebuild the play-by-play of important conversations with quotes, not just conclusions.
- **Book structure hints (teacher page)** — Chapter 1: turning bad questions into good ones; Chapter 2: three causes of bad data; Chapter 3: "important questions" and the big three learning goals; Chapter 5: commitment and advancement.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When you plan a discovery interview, write your questions and strike any that mention your solution or ask about the future ("would you", "how much would you pay"), replacing them with "tell me about the last time you…", because only past behavior predicts what people do.
- When an interviewee describes a problem, ask what they have already done about it and what it cost (time, money, tools, workarounds), because a problem nobody has tried to solve is usually not worth building for.
- When you hear a compliment, an opinion or a hypothetical, note it as noise and steer back to specifics ("When did that last happen? What did you do?"), because these feel like progress but carry no evidence.
- When someone requests a feature, ask what it would let them do and how they cope today before writing a ticket, because the request is a pointer to a motivation, not a specification.
- When you must show a prototype, finish the problem conversation first, then ask permission to switch to the product, because once the idea is on the table the person will start being polite.
- When a meeting ends with "this is great, keep me posted", ask for a concrete next step on the spot (a follow-up with a set goal, an intro to the decision-maker, a pilot with real data, a pre-order), because declining a real ask is the only honest no you'll get.
- When you run usability tests on a concept, don't treat "I'd use this" or "I like it" as validation of demand; measure demand with behavior (sign-ups with effort, deposits, repeat use), because usability and desirability are different questions (see `references/process/research-methods-selector.md`).
- When results are split (some care a lot, most don't), compare those who cared by context, role, worldview and constraints, then recruit more like them, because the signal is usually a hidden segment, not a small market.
- When feedback conflicts across user groups, decide which segment you serve and filter feedback by it, because requests from almost-but-not-quite-ideal users can drag the product off course.
- When the product creates a new behavior (no current workaround to ask about), get a rough prototype into hands early and watch what people do, because interviews can't validate a habit that doesn't exist yet.
- When synthesizing interviews, share exact quotes and the sequence of the conversation with the team, tagging good vs bad data, because summaries quietly turn compliments into findings.
- When interviews are forced into formal settings (group calls, procurement meetings), lower your confidence in what you hear and look for the clean facts within it, because the setting adds bias you can't control.
- When deciding whether to keep interviewing, ask whether more conversations could change the decision; if not, build the cheapest real thing, because learning has diminishing returns and some risks only show up in use.
- When a meeting is clearly the wrong one, end it early and give the time back, because respecting people's time builds the goodwill that gets you the right introductions.

## Evidence & caveats
- Practitioner advice from a founder, drawn from his own failed and successful companies. No studies; the gym and university examples are anecdotes.
- The core rule (ask about past behavior, not hypothetical future behavior) matches research methods practice and has support in research on poor prediction of one's own behavior and on intention–behavior gaps (see `references/books/paying-not-to-go-to-the-gym.md`, where gym members overestimated their attendance). That convergence makes the rule stronger than the book's own evidence.
- The book targets founders validating business ideas and B2B sales; designers should translate "commitment" into proportional behavioral signals (time in a pilot, data uploaded, a calendar invite), not cash.
- "Everyone is lying" is rhetorical. People aren't lying so much as being kind and bad at forecasting themselves.
- Fitzpatrick himself warns against treating conversations as the only tool and against chasing zero bias.
- He is writing a 2nd edition (per robfitz.com); the free material may reflect newer thinking than the 2013 text.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/process/research-methods-selector.md` §4 already summarizes the three rules and bad-data types; these notes add commitment/advancement, segmentation, and when not to rely on interviews. No conflict.
- Jobs-to-be-Done interviewing (Moesta's switch interviews, cited in the same file) also anchors on a specific past purchase; the two are complementary: JTBD maps forces of the decision, Mom Test filters for honest data and ends in a commitment.
- `references/books/microinteractions-saffer.md` (Saffer's "polish instead of MVP") implies investing in detail before demand is proven; Fitzpatrick would validate the problem first. Validate demand first, then polish the critical path.
- Survey-based demand questions ("How likely are you to use…?") in `references/data-driven/metrics-frameworks.md` measure attitude, not behavior; Fitzpatrick would discount them for go/no-go decisions. Use them for tracking sentiment, not validating demand.
