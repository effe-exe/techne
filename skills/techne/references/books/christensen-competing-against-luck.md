# Competing Against Luck — Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, David S. Duncan

Use this when: a team segments users by demographics or persona and still can't explain why people buy, switch or churn; you need to define who you really compete with (including "doing nothing"); you are choosing which product improvements matter and which are irrelevant to what users came for.

Basis: the book (HarperBusiness, 2016) was not read. These notes rest on Christensen's own free material that carries the book's theory: the full text of his 2007 *MIT Sloan Management Review* article "Finding the Right Job for Your Product" (Christensen, Anthony, Berstell, Nitterhouse), which is free on the publisher's site and is the direct forerunner of the book; the full transcript of his 2016 HBR IdeaCast interview about the book; his 2011 HBS Working Knowledge interview on jobs-to-be-done marketing; a JTBD Radio episode transcript in which he explains the theory to Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek; and the Christensen Institute's official Jobs to Be Done page. Coverage of the core theory, the milkshake, IKEA, OnStar and Kodak cases and the research method is good. The book's later chapters on organizing a company around jobs, its "fallacies of innovation data" and its full case set (SNHU, QuickBooks, Depends) are covered only thinly.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Innovation looks like luck because companies know a great deal about who their customers are and what their products do, but they don't know what causes a purchase. The cause is a "job": the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance, which has functional, emotional and social dimensions and leads them to "hire" a product (and "fire" another). If you understand the job, you can see who you really compete with, which improvements matter, which experiences of buying and using you must provide, and how to organize the company around delivering them.

## Core concepts
- **Job to be done** — the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. The 2007 article puts it as the fundamental problem a customer needs to resolve in a given situation. The circumstance, not the customer, is the unit of analysis.
- **Causation over correlation** — being 18–35 with a degree may correlate with buying, but it doesn't cause the purchase. A job that arises in someone's life does. Demographics and product categories are used because that data is easy to get, not because markets are structured that way.
- **Hire and fire** — customers bring products into their lives to do a job, and drop them when something does it better. The same person hires different products for different jobs at different times of day.
- **The milkshake** — early-morning buyers hired a thick shake to make a boring commute bearable and stay full until mid-morning, with one free hand. The competitors were bagels, bananas, donuts and boredom, not other milkshakes. Afternoon buyers (parents treating children) had a different job. Improving taste and thickness for "the milkshake customer" did nothing because those were the wrong dimensions.
- **Functional, emotional, social dimensions** — every job has all three. Emotions come from situations ("products don't engender emotions; situations do").
- **Job candidates are the real competition** — job-defined markets are larger than product-category markets. The free *Metro* newspaper competed with conversation, paperbacks, phones and boredom.
- **Nonconsumption** — people who want to make progress but hire nothing because the available options are too costly, complex or unknown. This is often the largest market, and where disruptions start.
- **Experiences in purchase and use** — once you know the job, the next questions are which experiences you must provide to do it perfectly, and what you must integrate to provide them. Differentiation that lasts comes from those experiences, not from a better feature list, which is easy to copy.
- **Escaping the positioning treadmill** — competing on axes such as selection or quality leads to feature copying and commoditization. IKEA built around "furnish my place today" (in-stock flat packs, take-home, utensils, child care), a combination that is hard to copy.
- **Purpose brand** — a brand that pops into mind when the job arises (FedEx, OnStar, Sawzall, Craigslist). It guides customers to the product and guides the company on what not to build. The price premium is the reward for doing the job.
- **Finding jobs** — look first at current customers (who use the product for unintended jobs and force-fit it), then at competitors' customers, then at nonconsumers. For businesses, the job is usually how the customer makes money. Methods: interviews about real purchase situations, observation of compensating behaviors (the dustpan that led to Swiffer), empathic immersion (Hill-Rom staff working as orderlies) and, for new technologies, co-evolving with early users (OnStar).
- **Situation cases** — write each purchase as a chronological story of events and thoughts. About 25 cases make critical mass. Cluster them by similar situation, then build a grid of job candidates against required experiences, and read off the "help wanted" signs, the unmet constraints.
- **Organizing around the job** (IdeaCast) — successful companies start focused on one job, then drift into serving "our customers' other jobs", which arise at different times. Christensen's examples are newspapers bundling classifieds and Citibank bundling financial products. Focused competitors then disrupt them.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a product or feature underperforms, don't ask users what to improve. Find the moments when it was actually hired: when, where, with whom, what else they did. Then ask what else they hire in that situation. Improve the dimensions that matter to that job.
- Write every opportunity as a circumstance plus progress ("when I'm commuting 30+ minutes alone at 7am, help me stay engaged and not be hungry before lunch"), never as a demographic ("busy professionals 25–40"). If two very different people hire the product for the same reason, they belong to the same segment.
- List the real job candidates, including non-digital workarounds and doing nothing. A to-do app competes with paper, email-to-self and memory. A meditation app competes with scrolling, wine and podcasts. Benchmark your onboarding and messaging against those, not only against the category leader.
- Look for compensating behaviors in research and analytics: exports to spreadsheets, copy-paste between tools, screenshots, sticky notes, users repurposing a feature. Each one is a job your product does only partly.
- Before adding features, build a grid: job candidates down the side, the experiences the job needs across the top (speed, confidence, social safety, effort), and how well each candidate does each. Build where your product is weak on an experience the job needs most. Ignore improvements that no job asks for.
- Design the whole hire, not only the product: discovery, purchase, onboarding, first use and support. The milkshake fix included moving the machine to the front of the counter and adding a prepaid card. Purchase friction is part of doing the job.
- Name and position features after the job they do (a purpose brand), so users think of them when the circumstance comes up: "Pay a bill", not "Payments 2.0".
- When stakeholders want to bundle more products for "our existing users", check whether the jobs arise at the same moment. If they don't, bundling adds complexity without adding value, and a focused competitor can take the job.
- For a new technology without a clear job (AI features are a current example), release a flexible, cheap-to-change version early. Then watch the circumstances in which people sign up and use it, until the job becomes clear (the OnStar route).
- Size the opportunity by the job, not the category. Count people who struggle with the circumstance, including nonconsumers. A category-based estimate usually understates the market and the real competition.
- Pair job discovery with measurement. Once the job and the experiences it needs are defined, test changes with controlled experiments (`references/data-driven/experimentation-playbook.md`). Job stories produce hypotheses; they don't prove effects.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a theory built from case studies, and Christensen's own results are mostly self-reported. The milkshake company is disguised. The morning share is "40%" in the 2007 article and the 2011 interview but "about half" in the 2016 IdeaCast. The "7x" sales gain he mentions in the IdeaCast has no published data behind it. Treat the cases as illustrations, not evidence.
- The success-rate claims (P&G new-product success rising from 15% to 50% under jobs thinking, and disruptive strategies rising from 6% to 35%, combined to "60–70%", from JTBD Radio) are cited from memory, with no studies attached or controls described. Don't repeat them as facts.
- Kodak's EasyShare share rose from 8% to 28% (2007 article) and then fell to an unprofitable 12% after a reorganization, by the authors' own account. A well-chosen job didn't protect the business, so jobs thinking is necessary but not sufficient.
- Context (not used as a basis): Christensen's broader disruption theory has faced serious academic criticism, for example King & Baatartogtokh's 2015 *MIT Sloan Management Review* study, which found that few of the theory's canonical cases fit all its conditions, and Jill Lepore's 2014 *New Yorker* critique. That criticism targets disruption, not JTBD directly, but it is a reason to treat Christensen's predictive claims with care.
- JTBD has rival schools. Christensen and Moesta's "progress in a circumstance" (`references/books/moesta-demand-side-sales.md`) differs from Ulwick's outcome-driven innovation (jobs as functional processes with measurable desired outcomes). The book follows the first. Jobs defined loosely enough can explain any outcome after the fact, so write them specifically enough that they could turn out wrong.
- "About 25 situation cases" is a heuristic. Moesta's guide says patterns appear within 5–8 switch interviews per job. Both are qualitative saturation rules, not samples for estimating how common something is.
- The *strategy+business* review (Kinni, 2016) was read only for the book's case list (SNHU, QuickBooks, Depends, OnStar). It is secondary and opinion.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/process/process-playbook.md` §2 summarizes JTBD alongside opportunity solution trees and Shape Up. That's consistent. This file adds the competitive-set, experiences and purpose-brand parts. Its warning that JTBD under-weights aesthetics holds here too: the book rarely discusses visual or interaction quality.
- `references/books/continuous-discovery-habits-torres.md` frames opportunities as customer needs and desires heard in story-based interviews. It's compatible: a job is a well-framed top-level opportunity. Torres adds the weekly cadence and assumption testing that Christensen leaves out.
- Persona-led advice elsewhere (for example persona sections in `references/process/research-methods-selector.md`): Christensen rejects demographic personas as causes. Keep behavioral personas, which describe circumstances and goals, and drop demographic ones as a basis for design decisions.
- `references/books/thinking-fast-and-slow-kahneman.md` and the bias literature explain why people's stated reasons for buying are unreliable. They support JTBD's focus on reconstructing real events rather than asking "why did you buy?"
