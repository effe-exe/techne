# Super Normal: Sensations of the Ordinary — Naoto Fukasawa & Jasper Morrison

Use this when: deciding how "designed" a product should look versus how quietly it should work; choosing between a distinctive custom component and a familiar convention; critiquing a UI that demands attention it hasn't earned (novel controls, loud styling, decorative motion); defining a visual direction for daily-use tools.

Basis: the book (Lars Müller, 2007) is a photo catalogue of about 200 everyday objects chosen for the 2006 Tokyo exhibition, with short texts. These notes rely on the authors' own published words: Morrison's "Super Normal" essay (the book's manifesto text, hosted on his studio site), the Morrison–Fukasawa dialogue from Domus 894 (2006, also on his site), two earlier Morrison essays that set up the idea (1991, 1996), a 2018 Morrison interview, a long Morrison interview on atmosphere, and a 2013 Core77 interview with Fukasawa. The ~200 photographs and captions, which are most of the book, were not seen. The ideas are probably well covered; the curated examples aren't.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Design has become a competition to be noticed, and objects made to catch the eye usually live badly with us, so the better goal is the "super normal" object: new, but feeling familiar from the start because it distils the long-evolved essence of its type. An object becomes super normal through use, not at first sight; you find it when you notice which glass, chair or cutter you keep reaching for, and its value lies in the atmosphere it creates and how it performs, not in how it looks in a photo. Designers can't manufacture normality, but they can aim at it by holding back personal expression, studying anonymous and long-lived objects, and judging designs by how they will be to live with.

## Core concepts
- **Super normal** — (Fukasawa) a new design that captures the essence of what everyone already recognises as normal, so people expecting "design" react with "that's so normal". New but familiar from the beginning, drawing on a shared memory of the type.
- **Becoming normal happens; you can't make it happen** — both authors say normality is conferred by collective use over time. Designers can only aim at it.
- **Becomes super normal through use** — (Morrison) the judgement is made in daily use, often slowly ("it may take some time before you appreciate them"), not at first glance. Morrison's test object: heavy old wine glasses from a junk shop whose absence he feels at the table.
- **Atmosphere** — Morrison's central criterion: every object changes the atmosphere of the room or table it enters. Good objects improve it; attention-seeking ones disturb it "by providing too much presence".
- **Noticeability as a cost** — about 99% of design objects are meant to be noticed quickly (his estimate, not a measurement); Morrison now treats "a certain lack of noticeability" as a requirement.
- **Special vs normal** — "special" things demand attention for the wrong reasons and are usually less useful and less rewarding over time. Normal grew unselfconsciously and is fragile: once wiped out, it is hard to rebuild.
- **Summarise, don't break with, the history of form** — the super normal object comes from the evolutionary development of everyday types and "knows its place in the society of things".
- **Anonymous objects and ego** — (Morrison, 1996) objects with no known author often behave more naturally than "pedigree" ones, where the creator's ego has replaced some usefulness. Marketing's demand for USPs pushes individuality at the expense of the design.
- **The unimportance of form** — (Morrison, 1991) don't see the designer as a form-giver. Let form come from an idea, a process, a material, a function, or a borrowed everyday object; hijacking the obvious is economical and shows there is beauty in the ordinary.
- **Balance of looks, atmosphere and function** — (Morrison, 2018) design is about looks, but looks serve the object's atmospheric potential and performance. You keep the thing you reach for because of how it performs, not how it looks.
- **Natural selection of objects** — (Fukasawa) with ten good-looking chairs at home you end up sitting on the comfortable ones; that daily choice is "the acceptance of design".
- **Without Thought / design dissolving into behaviour** — (Fukasawa) behaviour comes from the body responding to its surroundings, not from step-by-step thought, so design should fit those unconscious responses (the worn handholds on a climbing route).
- **Stimulation tires quickly** — (Fukasawa) be wary of "exciting" design; people tire of stimulation soon.
- **Global stock, local seasoning** — (Fukasawa) the body-level basis of design is shared worldwide like a soup stock; culture and branding are local seasoning.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a component has a well-established convention (text field, checkbox, tab bar, date picker, back button), start from the convention and refine it, because a super normal control is familiar from the first use. Invent only where the convention fails a real job, and measure the learning cost.
- When a review praises a screen for being "striking", ask how it will feel on the 200th use, because the authors judge through use and see high presence as a cost in daily-use tools. Put loudness (colour, motion, odd shapes) in moments of meaning and brand surfaces, not in the work area.
- When choosing between two designs, test them in repeated use (diary study, a week-long dogfood, a usability benchmark on the 3rd–5th task), not only first impressions or a static preference poll, because the authors' criterion is which object people keep choosing. First-glance preference tests reward noticeability.
- When you need a signal of quality for an everyday tool, look at retention and repeat choice (what users default to when several options exist: the chair they actually sit on), not compliments in a demo.
- When starting a visual direction, collect "anonymous" references from the product category (the default controls of mature OSs, long-lived tools, utilitarian signage, spreadsheets) as well as award galleries, because long-evolved types carry solved problems the author-led showcase pieces often drop.
- When adding a decorative element, ask whether it serves performance or the atmosphere of the whole screen. If it does neither, cut it: in Morrison's terms it is ego, not service.
- When a set of components is designed together (cards, buttons, icons, illustrations), avoid over-theming every piece to match, because Morrison argues a table looks contrived when glass, plate and cutlery share obvious visual motifs. Shared tokens for coherence; each component shaped by its own job.
- When a form or layout decision feels arbitrary, let it come from the content, data, platform or interaction (Morrison's "visual consequence of an idea, a process, a material, a function") rather than from a styling flourish.
- When users rely on a product daily, guard the "normal" they already have: existing positions, labels and behaviours. A redesign that wipes out a familiar normal is costly to rebuild; evolve instead (see `references/process/redesign-and-scale.md`).
- When briefing a brand or marketing team that wants a distinctive UI, separate the brand moment (onboarding, empty states, marketing pages) from the tool surface, which should recede. That is techne's "innovate in the core value, be conventional elsewhere".
- When designing for physical or embodied interaction (touch targets, gestures, thumb reach, device posture), observe what people do without thinking and design to that path, because Fukasawa's "Without Thought" holds that behaviour is the body responding to its surroundings. Observation and field study beat asking.
- When localising, keep the shared, body-level interaction model stable and adapt the "seasoning": content, imagery, tone, local conventions (Fukasawa's soup-stock metaphor).
- When "normal" would hide something users need (a cancel path, a risk warning, an AI limitation), make it visible anyway. Quietness is a default for daily use, not a licence to make important information hard to notice.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a designers' philosophy plus a curated exhibition, not research. There is no data in the material read; "99% of design objects" is rhetoric. Evidence rating: weak as stated, though parts line up with stronger findings.
- Convergent evidence: prototypicality raises aesthetic preference and first-impression appeal (see `references/books/tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md`, `references/books/hekkert-maya-typicality-novelty.md`), and familiarity reduces learning cost (Jakob's law in `references/books/laws-of-ux.md`). These support "familiar from the beginning", but the MAYA work shows people prefer typicality *with* some novelty, not pure normality.
- "Becomes super normal through use" is a plausible claim about mere exposure and habit (`references/books/good-habits-bad-habits-wood.md`), but nothing here measures it; a design chosen by repeated use may just be the default or the cheapest.
- Physical objects in a room aren't screens: a glass sits quietly on a shelf; a UI has to communicate state, affordance and feedback. Applied literally, "less noticeable" can strip signifiers (the flat-design problem noted in `references/visual/visual-polish-checklist.md`).
- Taste is involved: the selection is two famous designers' eye, with a Japanese/European sensibility (Fukasawa frames refinement and minimalism as one tradition among several). Don't treat it as universal.
- The book's photographs and captions were not read; examples beyond those named in interviews and essays (stools, wine glasses, box cutter, chairs) are not covered.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/books/hekkert-maya-typicality-novelty.md`: MAYA says aim for "most advanced yet acceptable", i.e. typicality plus novelty. Super Normal leans harder toward typicality. For tools used daily, follow Super Normal; for brand, launch and differentiation surfaces, MAYA's balance applies.
- `references/books/emotional-design-norman.md`: Norman gives visceral appeal real weight in experience. Morrison treats first-glance appeal as a trap. Both hold: visceral appeal affects first impressions and trust, while long-term preference follows performance. Measure both.
- `references/books/dribbblisation-of-design.md` and `references/masters/timeless-principles.md` (convention vs invention) agree with Super Normal; no conflict.
- `references/books/hooked-eyal.md` designs products to be noticed and returned to through triggers. Super Normal's ideal is the object people return to because it works, without prompting. When they conflict, prefer earned repeat use over engineered triggers (see `references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`).
