# The Principles of Psychology — William James

Use this when: you are designing for habitual use (keeping controls stable, avoiding action slips, launching a new behavior); deciding how to get and hold attention without wearing it out; designing learning, recall and naming; explaining why users and designers see what they expect to see (proofreading, first impressions, new product categories).

Source: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/ (Classics in the History of Psychology). Read in full: ch. IV Habit, ch. XI Attention, ch. XVI Memory (main text; footnotes read up to note 31), ch. XIX The Perception of 'Things' (main text and footnotes). Not read: the other 24 chapters, including IX (Stream of Thought), XV (Perception of Time) and XXVI (Will), which these chapters cross-reference.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
The nervous system is plastic: repeated actions wear paths that make the next repetition easier, more accurate, less tiring and less conscious, so habit frees the mind for new work but also fixes us in old ways and fires off the wrong routine when a familiar cue appears. Experience is what we attend to, attention is selective and can only be held voluntarily for seconds at a time, and what we notice depends on what we are already prepared to perceive. Memory and perception both run on the same machinery of association: a fact is retained in proportion to how many other things it is linked to, and we perceive not raw sensations but the most probable thing those sensations have meant before.

## Core concepts
**Habit (ch. IV)**
- **Plasticity** — a structure weak enough to yield to an influence, strong enough not to yield all at once. Repetition deepens paths.
- **What habit does** — simplifies the movement, makes it more accurate, reduces fatigue, and reduces the conscious attention needed. A learned chain runs off from a single cue, each step triggered by the sensation of the previous step, not by a decision.
- **Action slips** — the wrong habitual chain completes when it shares opening steps: winding a watch when taking off a waistcoat in the daytime; James walking to a house he'd lived in ten years before.
- **Habit knowledge is not declarative** — few people can say which sock or trouser leg goes on first or which way their door opens, yet their hands never err.
- **Habit as social flywheel** — it keeps people in their roles and patterns; by around 30, he claims, character has set like plaster (opinion).
- **Maxims for forming habits (via Bain)** — launch with as strong and decided a start as possible; never allow an exception until the habit is rooted; seize the first opportunity to act on every resolution; keep the capacity for effort alive with a little gratuitous practice each day. Early success matters because early failure dampens later attempts.

**Attention (ch. XI)**
- **Selective interest** — of the millions of things present to the senses, only what we notice shapes the mind; interest gives foreground and background.
- **Span** — Jevons, estimating beans at a glance: 3 and 4 always right, 5 right 102 of 107 times, 6 right 120 of 147. Grouping lets you take in as many groups as you could units. Cattell: 4–5 lines flashed for 1/100 s seen without error; about three times as many letters when they form words, and twice as many words when they form a sentence.
- **Doing two things at once** — two easy and different tasks can run together (Paulhan); two tasks of the same kind interfere; more than one non-habitual process requires rapid switching with no time gain.
- **Kinds of attention** — sensory vs intellectual; immediate (interesting in itself: intense, sudden, moving, strange, bright stimuli) vs derived (borrows interest from an associated interest, like a lover's tap on the window or your own name); passive vs voluntary.
- **Voluntary attention lasts seconds** — what we call sustained attention is repeated effort bringing the topic back. The object must change or develop; an unchanging object fades from attention (Helmholtz's retinal rivalry).
- **Effects of attention** — we perceive, distinguish and remember attended things better, and react faster.
- **Preparation and reaction time** — a warning signal shortens reaction time; uncertainty about the stimulus's strength or type lengthens it; an unexpectedly weak sound can push reaction time to 0.4–0.5 s. Wundt's table: unexpectedly strong sound +0.073 s, unexpectedly weak +0.171 s, interference by a like stimulus +0.045 s, by an unlike one +0.078 s. Münsterberg: reacting was much faster when attention was set on the answer than on the question.
- **Preperception** — attention includes an inner image of what you expect; we see what we have been prepared or taught to see, and once a feature is pointed out we notice it every time after.
- **Inattention** — constant stimuli (a ticking clock, the din of a factory) drop out of consciousness; their *stopping* is noticed.

**Memory (ch. XVI)**
- **Primary memory** — the just-past lingers for a few seconds without being recalled; fleeting "transitive" states are not remembered.
- **Retention = associations** — memory needs paths from a present cue to the event and its setting, so the more things a fact is linked to, the better it is retained. The secret of a good memory is forming many associations, i.e. thinking about the fact.
- **Cramming fails** — material learned for one occasion has few links and fades; material met day after day in varied contexts sticks.
- **Native retentiveness can't be trained** — practice improves methods, not raw retention. His own test: learning poetry took 50 s per line before a month of memory training and 57 s after; colleagues' results were mixed.
- **Recency, attention, repetition** improve memory; too many varied contexts give familiarity without a specific memory ("I know it, but not from where").
- **Forgetting is necessary** — without dropping most detail we could not recall at all.
- **Proper names go first** — concrete names have fewer associations than general words, so they are the hardest to recall.
- **Active recall beats rereading** — when you almost know a piece, recalling it from within fixes it better than looking at the book again. We also remember ideas we generated better than ones handed to us.
- **Retaining for a deadline** — people can hold something until it's used and then let it fade (the barrister's case details).

**Perception of things (ch. XIX)**
- **Perception = sensation + suggestion** — we perceive the probable thing that a sensation has usually meant. Illusions come from the inferred part, not the sensation.
- **Two types of illusion** — the habitual cause is not the actual one (crossed-finger pea, train-window motion), or the mind is full of a thing and finds it everywhere (the hunter's woodcock, the proofreader's illusion).
- **Proofreader's illusion** — fluent readers supply much of each word from their own heads, so they miss typos that a less fluent reader catches.
- **Unfamiliar views reveal raw qualities** — a landscape seen upside down or an inverted painting shows color, contrast and balance more freshly, at the cost of meaning; a word stared at long enough loses its meaning.
- **Assimilation to old heads** — new things are filed under the nearest existing concept (horses called pigs, an orange called a ball). We feel curiosity only about things we partly know; what violates our habits of perception is often ignored.
- **Reading speed** — among practiced readers, Romanes found a 4:1 difference in reading speed, with the fastest readers also retaining most.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a product is used habitually, keep the position, order and look of top actions stable, because habitual chains run off from the sensation of the previous step; moving a step breaks the chain even when the new layout is "better".
- When two flows start the same way but end differently (archive vs delete, send vs schedule), make them diverge early and visibly, because the more habitual chain completes itself when the first steps match (James's watch-winding slip).
- When you research habitual tasks, observe rather than ask: people can't report the order of their own habitual actions. Use logs, contextual inquiry or session replay.
- When launching a new habit or workflow, give users an immediate first chance to act on their intent and an early success, because James's maxims put the decisive start and the first opportunity first.
- When users show intent (a "remind me" tap, an emotional moment in content), put the matching action right there; James argues that a resolve that evaporates without action makes later action less likely.
- When users make the same decision over and over (format, destination, default), let them hand it to the product (defaults, "always do this"), because offloading routine decisions to automatism frees attention for real work.
- When you need users to take in a count at a glance, keep it to 4 or fewer items, or group larger sets into clusters of 2–5 (Jevons: 3–4 never wrong, 6 already wrong 18% of the time).
- When showing short text for a glance (notifications, buttons, signs), use real words and phrases rather than codes; people take in about three times more letters when they form words.
- When asking users to do two things at once (watch a video and fill a form, follow voice directions while reading), combine different, easy channels. Never ask for two tasks of the same kind (two reading tasks) at once.
- When content needs long attention (tutorials, long reads, dashboards), make it develop: each screen or section should add something new. Static content stops being seen, however important.
- When something must interrupt, a sudden, intense, moving or personally relevant stimulus will win (the user's name, their own item). Reserve these for things that matter, and expect constant badges and banners to fade into inattention like the ticking clock.
- When feedback or alerts appear, make their timing and location predictable. Unexpected strength, type or timing measurably slows response (+0.07 to +0.17 s in Wundt's data) and startles.
- When users choose from a known set (command palettes, quick replies), show the options before they need them, because attention set toward the answer gave much faster responses than attention on the question.
- When introducing a feature users don't notice, point it out once with a name and an example. Preperception means people who have been shown a feature see it every time after; people who haven't may never discover it.
- When you want something remembered, connect it to things the user already knows and uses, and let them meet it in several contexts over days; one big tour is cramming.
- When teaching, ask users to recall or try before showing the answer (retrieval), and let them build or configure things themselves, because active recall and self-generated ideas are retained better.
- When labeling and naming, don't rely on users remembering proper names you invented (feature names, plan names); pair them with descriptive words and icons, because proper names are the first thing memory loses.
- When proofreading your own UI copy, use fresh eyes or tools: fluent authors supply words from their heads and miss errors. Have someone unfamiliar with the text check it, or read it backwards or out of context.
- When judging a layout's visual balance, flip or invert the screen (or view it upside down), which strips meaning and exposes color, contrast and balance problems, as James describes for inverted paintings.
- When introducing a new product category, describe it through the nearest familiar concept users already have, then show how it differs; people assimilate the new under old heads and ignore what fits none.
- When a large area of the screen moves together (parallax, full-screen transitions), expect some users to feel that *they* are moving (James's train-window illusion); keep large-field motion subtle and honor reduced-motion settings.

## Evidence & caveats
- Mixed. James mostly combines introspection, anecdote and summaries of others' experiments (Wundt, Exner, Helmholtz, Cattell, Ebbinghaus); he reports few controlled studies of his own (the memory-training test is small and informal).
- Held up well: subitizing up to about 4, chunking, habituation to constant stimuli, divided-attention costs for similar tasks, expectancy effects on perception, the testing (retrieval practice) and generation effects, spacing over cramming, and capture errors in habitual action (later formalized by Norman and Reason).
- Dated or wrong: the brain-path physiology is speculation; "character set like plaster by 30" and the never-allow-an-exception maxim are opinion; the claim that native retentiveness can't change rests on thin data. Some passages contain class, gender and colonial prejudice ("barbarian nations") that has no scientific value.
- Reaction-time numbers are 19th-century lab values from a few trained observers; use them for direction, not thresholds.

## Disagreements with other references
- James's (via Bain) maxim "never suffer an exception" conflicts with `references/psychology/cognition-emotion-experience-habit.md` §8, which cites Lally 2010: missing a single day did not materially derail habit formation. Trust Lally (data over maxim): design streak freezes and forgiving recovery rather than all-or-nothing streaks.
- `SKILL.md` §2 and `references/psychology/cognition-cheatsheet.md` give working memory as ~4 chunks; James's span data (Jevons, Cattell) agree with 4 for unrelated items and show grouping raises the effective number. No conflict.
- `references/psychology/cognition-perception-attention-reading.md` §5 covers change blindness and banner blindness with modern evidence; James's inattention and preperception are the older account of the same phenomena and add one design point: the *stopping* of a constant stimulus is noticed, so removing a long-standing element is more noticeable than adding one.
- `references/books/ebbinghaus-memory.md`: James summarizes Ebbinghaus accurately but rounds: he says a full half of the forgetting happens within the first half-hour, while the table shows about 42% forgotten at 20 minutes and 56% at one hour. Close enough for direction; use Ebbinghaus's table for numbers.
