# Of the Standard of Taste — David Hume

Use this when: someone says "design is subjective, so any choice is as good as another"; you need to decide whose judgment to trust in a design review (or how to make your own judgment trustworthy); you are critiquing a design made for an audience, era or culture that is not yours; two good options remain and the team is split along personal preference lines.

Source: https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/st (Hume Texts Online, edited text of Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary, Essay XXIII). Legal basis: public domain (published 1757); free scholarly web edition. Read in full.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Tastes plainly differ, and beauty is a feeling in the observer rather than a property of the object, yet everyone also knows some judgments are absurd (no one seriously ranks a hack writer with Milton), so "there's no disputing taste" cannot be the whole story. Hume's resolution: certain qualities of objects are fitted by human nature to please, the rules of art are just generalisations from experience of what has pleased across nations and ages, and the true standard is the joint verdict of rare critics whose perception is not impaired. Those critics combine five traits (delicacy, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense), and residual disagreement that comes from temperament or culture, with no defect on either side, is legitimate and cannot be settled.

## Core concepts
- **Sentiment vs judgment** — opinions about facts can be right or wrong; feelings are always "real" to the person who has them. Hume grants this, then argues it does not make all tastes equal.
- **Rules are empirical** — rules of composition are not derived from reason a priori; they are observations of what has reliably pleased people everywhere. A work can please in spite of breaking rules (because of other strengths), never because of the breakage; if the "fault" itself pleases, the rule was wrong.
- **The durability test** — fashions, authority and prejudice can give a bad work a temporary vogue, but works that keep pleasing across centuries, languages and cultures reveal what human nature actually responds to. Abstract theories rise and fall faster than admired works.
- **Fragile conditions of judging** — fine feelings need the right conditions: calm mind, focused attention, fitting time and place. Judge in a hurry or distracted and the "experiment" is invalid.
- **Delicacy** — the ability to detect small qualities mixed into a whole (Sancho's kinsmen tasting leather and iron in the wine, vindicated by the key on a leather thong at the bottom of the barrel). Shared, explicit principles and acknowledged models are what let you *prove* a fine judgment to a sceptic.
- **Practice** — repeated exposure to one kind of beauty turns a confused overall impression into a clear perception of each part's merits; a single first viewing is clouded by a "flutter" of thought, and showy, superficial beauty pleases at first and then palls.
- **Comparison** — you can only assign degrees of praise by having seen many works of different quality; someone who has seen only crude work will over-admire it.
- **Freedom from prejudice** — a judge must take the point of view the work assumes (its intended audience), and set aside friendship, rivalry and their own situation.
- **Good sense** — reason checks prejudice, grasps how parts relate to the whole, and judges how well the means fit the work's purpose (persuade, instruct, please).
- **The true judge** — strong sense, delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, cleared of prejudice; their *joint* verdict is the standard. Whether a given person has these traits is a factual, arguable question.
- **Legitimate variation** — two sources of disagreement remain that no standard can settle: individual temperament (age, preference for the sublime vs the tender, correctness vs energy) and the customs of one's own era and country. Preferences of that kind are "innocent and unavoidable".
- **Moral limit** — differences of custom (clothing, manners) deserve allowance; works that present vice without disapproval are genuinely flawed, and the audience cannot be asked to switch off its moral sense.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a stakeholder says "it's all subjective", separate the disagreement into (a) factual or functional claims that can be tested and (b) residual preference, because Hume shows most quality disputes contain a checkable core and only a small remainder is legitimate taste.
- When deciding whose critique to weight, ask which reviewers have the five traits for *this* domain (detail perception, lots of practice in this kind of product, broad comparison set, no stake in the outcome, understanding of the product's purpose), because seniority or confidence is not the standard; the traits are.
- When you critique, judge against the product's purpose and intended user first ("is this fit for its end?"), because Hume makes fitness to purpose a requirement of good taste, not an alternative to it.
- When reviewing a design, look at it more than once, in different conditions (fresh eyes, other device, other day), and never decide from a hurried first glance in a meeting, because first impressions are confused and favour showy, superficial appeal that soon wears off.
- When building your taste (or an agent's), pair volume of exposure with deliberate side-by-side comparison against acknowledged excellent references, because practice sharpens perception and comparison calibrates the scale of praise.
- When you need to convince someone of a fine-grained flaw, name the principle, show an accepted example where the principle clearly applies, then show it applies here, because explicit principles plus models are how a subtle judgment becomes demonstrable (the key with the leather thong).
- When designing for an audience unlike you (other culture, age, profession), deliberately take their point of view before judging, because a critic who keeps their own standpoint "rashly condemns" what works for the intended audience.
- When two well-made options differ only in temperament (calm vs energetic, minimal vs rich), stop arguing taste and decide by brand point of view or user evidence, because no standard can settle blameless differences of disposition.
- When you have a team split on taste, prefer a pooled verdict of several qualified reviewers over any single one, because Hume's standard is a joint verdict, which averages out individual quirks.
- When a "rule" (grid, convention, heuristic) is broken and the result still works, check whether it works because of the break or in spite of it, because only the first case is a reason to revise the rule.
- When choosing references for inspiration, favour work that has stayed admired across years and contexts over this season's trend, because durable admiration is the best signal of what human nature actually responds to.
- When you write a critique, state which of the five traits limits you on this piece (e.g., "I have little comparison base for medical dashboards", "I built the old version"), because naming your own weak spot lets others weight your verdict honestly.
- When a reviewer praises a minor flourish over the core experience, ask what they are comparing it to, because Hume notes that judges with no comparison base admire "frivolous beauties" that experienced judges treat as defects.
- When you are too close to your own work (sunk effort, authorship), get a verdict from someone with no stake, because self-interest is a form of the prejudice that Hume says disqualifies a judge.
- When a design relies on a cultural convention your team doesn't share (date formats, colour meanings, directionality, imagery), accept it as an innocent difference of custom and judge the design inside that convention, because faulting it is "false delicacy".
- When a design normalises something harmful to users (manipulation, exclusion), treat it as a real flaw even if it is the local norm, because Hume draws the line at customs that confuse right and wrong.

**How to run a "Hume panel" in a design review:**
1. Pick 3–5 reviewers and score each on the five traits for this domain; drop anyone with a stake in a specific option.
2. Set the conditions: calm session, full attention, real device and real content, the intended user and purpose written at the top.
3. Each reviewer looks twice (first pass, then after a break) and writes a verdict with reasons before discussion, so first-speaker bias doesn't dominate.
4. Put the candidates next to 2–3 acknowledged excellent references for the same job.
5. Sort disagreements into: factual or functional (send to a test or usability session), principle-based (show the principle and an example), and temperament (decide by brand point of view).
6. Record the pooled verdict and the cheapest behavioural check that could overturn it.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a philosophical essay (1757), not an empirical study: the five traits are argued from analogy and examples, not measured. Weight it as a well-reasoned framework for critique, not as evidence about what users prefer.
- Modern empirical aesthetics partly supports it: expertise does change aesthetic perception and agreement among trained judges is higher than among novices, and fluency/prototypicality research (see `references/books/tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md`) shows widely shared responses. But it also shows that experts and lay users often disagree, which matters because products serve lay users.
- The "test of time" is survivorship-biased and slow; it tells you little about a new interface next quarter. Use it for choosing references, not for validating your own design.
- Hume's examples carry 18th-century European bias (he dismisses non-European works and religions in ways we would not accept), an instance of the very prejudice his framework warns against. Keep the method, discard the examples.
- "True judges are easy to recognise" is optimistic. In practice, define the traits explicitly and calibrate reviewers against user data.
- For products, the ultimate arbiter is behaviour of the intended users, not expert consensus: Hume's critic proposes, experiments decide.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/masters/taste.md` already summarises Hume's five traits; this file adds the argument behind them (sentiment vs judgment, durability, the fragile conditions for judging, legitimate variation) and concrete review heuristics. They agree; treat taste.md as the summary and this as the source.
- `references/books/taste-for-makers.md` (Paul Graham) also argues taste can be good or bad, but grounds it in traits of the work (simple, timeless, solves the right problem); Hume grounds it in traits of the judge. Use Graham's list to critique the artifact and Hume's list to decide whose critique counts.
- techne's evidence hierarchy (`SKILL.md` §2) ranks expert opinion below user behaviour. Hume would put qualified critics at the top for questions of beauty. Resolve by scope: for functional quality and business outcomes, trust behavioural data; for craft quality among options that test equal, use a pooled verdict of qualified reviewers.
- Bourdieu (cited in `references/masters/taste.md`) would say Hume's "true judge" is a social-class position dressed up as perception. Take this as a warning: check that "good taste" in your team isn't just in-group style.
