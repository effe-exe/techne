# Everything is a Remix — Kirby Ferguson

Use this when: a team is stuck waiting for an "original" idea; you are using references (Mobbin, Dribbble, competitors) and need rules for copying well; you are deciding whether to follow a convention or break it; you are judging whether a design copies too much from one source; you are framing the ethics of AI-generated design work trained on others' material.

Source: https://www.everythingisaremix.info/ (author's site). Read: the full official transcript of *Everything is a Remix: The Complete, Updated 2023 Edition* (Parts 1–4: music, film and memes, the elements of creativity, artificial creativity) at /everything-is-a-remix-2023; the official TED transcript of Ferguson's linked talk "Embrace the Remix" (TED 2012, ted.com); and four short site articles ("Why Most Work Feels Empty and How to Fix It", "Imagination Isn't Algorithmic", "Wrestling with Originality", "Poster for The Sting (1973) vs The Studio (2025)"). Not read: the original 2010–2012 episodes as separate videos (no official transcripts found), the paid courses, and other site articles. The 2023 edition replaces and updates the original series, but the original's Part 4 on intellectual property is represented only through the TED talk.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Creativity is not a lightning bolt from a lone genius but the application of three ordinary tools to existing material: copy (to become fluent), transform (to make variations), and combine (to bring ideas from different places together), which is where breakthroughs come from. People love "more of the same but different" because we understand new things through old ones (Hofstadter's analogy), so genres, conventions and sequels dominate, and even celebrated originals like *Star Wars* and *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* are new combinations of borrowed parts. Copying has bad forms (too much from one place, uncredited, possessive double standards), and law and loss aversion make us protect our own ideas while freely using others'; in the AI era Ferguson argues machines can synthesize but not create meaning, so humans should own the *why*.

## Core concepts
- **Remix** — "to copy, transform and combine existing materials to produce something new".
- **Copy** — the foundation: "We can't introduce anything new until we're fluent in the language of our domain, and we do that by copying." Examples: Minecraft began as an "Infiniminer clone"; Linux as a UNIX clone; the Mac copied ideas from the Xerox Alto; Stephen King copied comics into notebooks.
- **Transform** — variations on an existing idea, "time-consuming tinkering". Product examples: Discord kept the chat feature from a failed game; Pinterest kept the clipping feature from a catalogue product; TikTok moved from lip-sync to short video.
- **Combine** — breakthroughs come from bringing together elements already around: Gutenberg's press, Ford's Model T (assembly line + interchangeable parts + the car), the Web (Berners-Lee combining established ideas), *Deus Ex* combining three genres, *Cuphead* combining 1930s animation with a run-and-gun game.
- **More of the same but different** — audiences prefer the familiar with variation: by Ferguson's count, 92 of the 100 yearly top-ten box office films from 2012–2021 were sequels, remakes or adaptations.
- **Genre as a contract** — a genre is a loose set of rules the audience expects; makers follow, twist or subvert them, but they "gotta do most".
- **Analogy as thinking** — citing Hofstadter, we "make sense of the new and unknown in terms of the old and known"; we understand new stories better when they resemble old ones.
- **Originality is the combination** — of *Into the Spider-Verse*: "You can't really point to anything in this movie that is original," yet the sum is original.
- **First we copy, then we create** — style emerges from copying what you love (Yohji Yamamoto: "At the end of the copy you will find yourself").
- **Bad copying** — copying too much from one source (the *Studio* poster takes everything around its central image from *The Sting*); failing to credit (Led Zeppelin's "Dazed and Confused" credit added only after a 2010 suit); taking signature work without permission (Fortnite emotes). "There's very clearly many many many bad ways to copy."
- **Loss aversion and IP** — "great artists steal, but not from me": Jobs quoted Picasso about stealing great ideas in 1996, then fought Android over copying in 2010. People protect what they feel is theirs and copy others freely; property-based IP law plus large settlements plus this bias produced the smartphone patent wars (TED talk).
- **Small-part patents** — the slide-to-unlock patent shows patents covering small details of long-evolving technology (multi-touch dates to Bill Buxton's work in the 1980s); Ferguson asks what would have happened if Xerox had patented pop-up menus and scrollbars.
- **Mods and players** — many genres and hits started as user modifications (DOTA as a Warcraft III map, *The Stanley Parable* as a Half-Life 2 mod, battle royale from ARMA 2 → DayZ → PUBG → Fortnite).
- **AI as remixer** — image generation copies at scale; Ferguson splits it into scraping (acceptable to him), training (ambiguous: is diffusion copying?) and generating (acceptable), concludes "not guilty" with caveats: opt-outs for everyone, no training on individual artists' work, and a model trained only on public-domain and licensed images.
- **Derivative by design** — "AI is derivative by design and it is inventive by chance"; it synthesizes what we know without understanding or lived experience. Meaning (purpose, belonging, storytelling, transcendence, after Emily Esfahani Smith) is the human part.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a team waits for an original idea, start by copying: rebuild 2–3 best-in-class flows for the same job (screen by screen, in Figma or code) before designing your own, because fluency in a domain's language comes from copying, and the original version comes after.
- When collecting references, collect from at least three sources and from outside the product category (games, print, film, physical tools), because breakthroughs come from combining; copying only from direct competitors gives you their average. This matches the rule of three+ (no source > ~40% of decisions) in `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md` §8.
- When your design looks very like one reference, check how much comes from that single source, and if most of the composition, palette or framing comes from it, transform further, because the *Sting*/*Studio* case is Ferguson's own example of copying "too much from one place".
- When designing a new interaction, borrow a familiar model users already know (inbox, cart, timeline, map, deck of cards) and change only what your product needs, because people understand new things through old ones; a familiar base with one twist is "more of the same but different".
- When deciding how far to break a convention, list the "genre rules" users expect for this category (e.g. checkout: cart summary, total before pay, guest option) and keep most of them, twisting only where you win, because genres have rules makers must mostly honour; see convention vs invention in `references/masters/timeless-principles.md`.
- When a product or feature underperforms, look in the usage data for the one part people love (a sub-feature, a workaround, a way of using it you didn't plan) and consider making it the core, as Discord, Pinterest and TikTok did, because transformation often comes from what users already chose.
- When users build mods, templates, workarounds or community extensions, treat them as a research and innovation pipeline (collect, credit, sometimes productize), because whole genres began as player modifications.
- When you borrow a distinctive element (a signature illustration style, a creator's motion, a specific interaction someone is known for), get permission or credit it, because uncredited and unauthorized copying is where remixing turns into wrongdoing.
- When your team is upset that a competitor copied you, check whether you've done the same, and compete on the combination and execution rather than on ownership of a pattern, because "great artists steal, but not from me" is loss aversion, not principle; generic patterns are free (see `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md` rule 7).
- When an AI tool generates design options, treat them as remixes of the common case and push for combinations the model wouldn't produce by default (unusual references, constraints from outside the category), because AI is "derivative by design"; protect the designer's own divergent ideas first (see `references/future/human-ai-cocreation.md`).
- When you use generative AI on brand or illustration work, prefer models or settings trained on licensed or public-domain material and avoid prompting in the style of a living individual artist, because even Ferguson, who defends AI image generation, says training on individual artists' work "does seem wrong".
- When presenting a direction, name its lineage (which references contributed what), because combination is the creative act, and showing it makes the transformation visible and credits the sources.
- When a junior designer is criticized for work that is too close to a reference, coach them to name what they copied and change the combination rather than banning references, because "first we copy, then we create" and young creators who go too far are still on the right path.
- When pitching a breakthrough concept, describe it as a specific combination of existing parts ("X's data model with Y's interaction and Z's pricing"), because that is how Ferguson explains the printing press, the Model T and the Web, and it makes feasibility easier to judge.
- When a novel feature is hard to explain, write its explanation as an analogy to something users already know, then test whether new users can say what it does, because analogy is how people make sense of the new.

## Evidence & caveats
- A popular documentary series and talk, not research. Examples are hand-picked successes (Star Wars, Fortnite, Spider-Verse), so survivorship bias is strong: many remixes and pivots failed and aren't shown.
- The box-office figure (92 of 100 top-ten films, 2012–2021) is Ferguson's own count; the claim that about two-thirds of Dylan's early melodies were borrowed is presented as an estimate (TED talk). Treat both as illustrative.
- The Hofstadter claim about analogy is cited from *Surfaces and Essences* (Hofstadter and Sander, 2013); it is a cognitive-science argument, consistent with research on analogical reasoning and processing fluency, but the series doesn't present data.
- "More of the same but different" fits the MAYA idea (most advanced yet acceptable) and the familiarity findings in `references/books/tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md` (prototypicality raises first-impression appeal). That convergence is moderate support for the general point, not for Ferguson's specific examples.
- Some statements are contestable: "it was Apple that actually invented the smartphone" is disputed (earlier touchscreen smartphones existed). Fortnite/emote and Zeppelin details are summarized from public cases.
- The AI section (2023) is opinion. Ferguson predicted diffusion training would be judged fair use; legal outcomes vary by jurisdiction and case and were not settled in the text. Don't treat his verdict as legal guidance.
- The TED talk (2012) uses "loss aversion" loosely as an explanation for IP behaviour; it is an analogy, not a measured application.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md` §8 cites Ferguson for "studying and recombining influences, not tracing" and sets rules (≥ 3 references, ≤ ~40% from one, change 3 of 5 signature dimensions). Ferguson is more permissive than those rules: he celebrates heavy sampling, mods and copying as learning, and defends unlicensed AI training. For shipped commercial work keep the stricter inspiration-method rules; use Ferguson's permissiveness for learning exercises and internal exploration.
- `references/books/taste-for-makers.md` ("good design can copy"; unknowing imitation copies an imitator) agrees with Ferguson that copying is how makers start. Graham adds a warning Ferguson underplays: copy from originals, not copies of copies. `references/masters/timeless-principles.md` (convention vs invention) supports Ferguson's "genre rules" point for functional surfaces.
- `references/future/human-ai-cocreation.md` warns that AI examples cause fixation and homogenize ideas. Ferguson's "derivative by design" agrees with the mechanism but is more optimistic about AI as a collaborator; follow the co-creation protocol (designer ideas first) when they conflict.
- `references/books/vitruvius-ten-books.md` condemns plagiarism harshly while openly compiling from predecessors with credit. Ferguson and Vitruvius agree that the line is attribution and transformation, not whether you borrowed.
- `references/psychology/behavior-persuasion-toolkit.md` treats loss aversion as a measured bias with contested magnitude; Ferguson's use of it for IP disputes is rhetorical.
