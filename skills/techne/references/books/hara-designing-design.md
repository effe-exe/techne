# Designing Design — Kenya Hara

Use this when: a brand, product or interface is drifting into decoration or "more features = more value" and you need a philosophy of restraint that isn't just minimalism; you are designing for the senses (material, texture, touch, haptics) rather than for screen-only form; you want a method for seeing ordinary, familiar things afresh (re-design, "making the known unknown") before redesigning them.

Basis: the book (Lars Müller Publishers, 2007; 472 pages, mostly images, out of print) was not read. These notes come from the publisher's official preview on its book page (20 page spreads: the full table of contents and the opening pages of the RE-DESIGN, HAPTIC, WHITE, MUJI and EXFORMATION chapters, plus pages on signage, the Matsuya Ginza identity, the "tip of Asia" essay and the Beijing Olympics logo proposal), read page by page; Hara's own emailed answers quoted in a 2009 *Metropolis* article about the book; and a 2017 *Dezeen* interview with him about MUJI and emptiness. Coverage is low. The ideas behind the chapter titles are clear, but most of the argument, the long chapters on MUJI and on "What is design?" (its history from the Industrial Revolution through modernism), and nearly all the examples are known only from headings. This file is a lens for taste and restraint, not a source of tested heuristics.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Design is less about making new things than about awakening people to the things and senses they already have: "making the known unknown" lets us see the ordinary freshly, and the resources for great design lie in everyday life (Re-Design, Haptic, Exformation). Hara's central aesthetic is emptiness rather than simplicity: an empty vessel (the Japanese flag, the shrine offering box, MUJI's horizon posters) accepts the many meanings people bring to it, so communication works best when it leaves room rather than delivering one fixed message. For MUJI this becomes a stance against appetite and excess: the goal is products that earn rational, calm satisfaction ("this will do") rather than desire ("this is what I want").

## Core concepts
- **Re-Design: making the ordinary unknown** — redesigning familiar objects (toilet paper, matches, entry stamps, diapers, tea bags) as if seeing them for the first time. Hara argues that making the known unknown is as creative as making something new. Shigeru Ban's square-core toilet paper (it stacks and dispenses with resistance) is the signature example.
- **Haptic: designing the senses** — the senses aren't passive "receptors" but active organs reaching out to the world. Design is "not how to create, but how to make someone sense something". Examples are Fukasawa's banana-skin Juice Skin packages and Suzuki's paper cabbage bowls, objects whose meaning is carried by touch and material.
- **Senseware** — media and materials (paper, fiber) that stimulate the senses and carry information through texture. The Matsuya Ginza identity uses one dot pattern across the building, bags, wrapping paper and point card, so touching the card recalls the walls.
- **Architecture of information** — perception and memory combine in the brain to build an "information architecture". Design adds stimuli to that structure, not just shapes on a surface (from the chapter headings and the Senseware text).
- **White** — white is not a color but a sensibility: an aspiration towards emptiness and purity that is always a little soiled in reality, and which makes things noticeable.
- **Emptiness vs simplicity** — in his words to *Metropolis*, Western simplicity is about 150–200 years old, arising once ornament no longer had to signal power, and means form and use coming closest together. Japanese emptiness (from the tea ceremony aesthetic of the late 15th century and the Ashikaga shogunate) is a deliberate, controlled space that lets people bring their own meaning.
- **The empty vessel in communication** — the Japanese flag or a shrine's offering box works because it accepts all interpretations. MUJI ads have no copy and put the product (or a horizon) centred with the logo. The advertising concept is to be a "capacious vessel" for the many reasons people like MUJI: ecology, price, aesthetics, habit.
- **Acceptance, not appetite** — MUJI should satisfy with "this will do" rather than provoke "this is what I want": rational, sufficient goods, an alternative to consumption driven by capital and desire. The chapter asks whether affluence might lie in understatement.
- **Levels of desire / education of desire** — products reflect the "level of desire" of the market they're designed for. Japanese cars, he argues, are mild and ego-less because they precisely mirror Japanese desires. Design can raise or educate desire, not just follow it.
- **Exformation** — the opposite of information: not making known but realizing how little we know. Students re-present a familiar subject (the Shimanto River, "resort") so it becomes unknown again: asphalt roads composited with river surfaces, a soft-serve cone shaped like its swirl. Raising questions matters more than giving answers.
- **MUJI as a question, not a trend** (*Dezeen*, 2017) — minimalism and simplicity are not MUJI's only features. It "is always changing" and works like water filling gaps. People go there without a specific item in mind.
- **Viewing the world from the tip of Asia** — Japan at the receiving end of the Silk Road "pachinko machine" absorbs and refines influences. A modest position at the edge, rather than the centre, enables a global outlook.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- Before redesigning a familiar screen or flow (login, settings, receipt, notification), do a "re-design" exercise. Describe it as if you'd never seen one, list what it actually does and what each part is for, then redesign from that description, not from the current pattern. Use it to find hidden assumptions, then check the result against conventions (`references/interaction/platform-conventions.md`) before shipping anything unconventional.
- Prefer restraint that leaves room for users' own meaning on brand and marketing surfaces: fewer claims, one strong image, the product centred. Don't treat it as a rule for task UIs, where users need explicit labels (see caveats).
- Separate "empty" from "minimal-and-confusing". Emptiness is deliberate, controlled space around a few well-chosen elements. Removing labels or affordances to look clean is not Hara's emptiness, and it fails usability.
- Design for touch and material where the product has a physical edge: packaging, print, haptics, sound, device feedback. Ask what someone should feel through their fingers, not just what they should see. In digital products this maps to haptic feedback patterns, texture in motion and sound (`references/interaction/microinteractions-and-motion.md`).
- Carry one sensory motif across every touchpoint (pattern, texture, tone), as Matsuya Ginza did from façade to point card, so each contact recalls the whole brand.
- Aim for "this will do" satisfaction in utility products. Optimize for calm sufficiency and trust rather than craving, especially in money, health and productivity apps. It also guards against attention-maximizing dark patterns (`references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`).
- When a team can't agree on a concept, run an "exformation" exercise. Take the thing everyone thinks they understand ("onboarding", "a dashboard") and present it in an unfamiliar form, to provoke questions before choosing answers.
- Let the medium itself carry the message when you can. Hara's Umeda Hospital signs were washable white cotton, so the signage itself communicated cleanliness. A digital equivalent: let a privacy feature's interaction itself feel private and contained, rather than adding a "we care about privacy" banner.
- Design wayfinding for the least able user in the real setting. In the Katta clinic, Hara used very large type set into the floor and walls for elderly patients, avoiding protruding sign structures. In apps, size and place navigation cues for the real context (older users, glare, one hand), not for the mockup.
- Treat packaging, error states and empty states as design opportunities, the "ordinary" surfaces Re-Design targets. They are seen often and rarely designed.
- When writing a brand brief, list the different reasons people choose you (cheap, ethical, calm, habitual) and check whether your messaging locks out all but one. If it does, consider a more open, "vessel" message that holds them all.
- Test restraint with real users. A five-second test or first-impression study (`references/books/tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md`) shows whether "empty" reads as calm quality or as unfinished.
- Use white and negative space as an active material for hierarchy and calm, not as leftover space (`references/visual/layout-spacing-elevation.md`).

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a design philosophy and portfolio, not research. Nothing here is tested. Its value is as a lens and as a source of taste. Treat its heuristics as hypotheses to prototype.
- "Emptiness lets people project their own meaning" is persuasive for brand communication, but an empty interface also gives no guidance. Usability evidence (Krug, Norman, Nielsen) favours explicit labels and signifiers on task surfaces. Restraint must never cost comprehension.
- Several claims are cultural generalizations (Japanese vs Western sensibility, "Japanese cars have no ego"). They are opinion and risk essentialism. Use them as a provocation, not as facts about users.
- The *Metropolis* article is a journalist's piece. Only the passages Hara wrote to the author, and the article's quotes from the book, were used as basis. The "this will do" passage is quoted there from the book, and the MUJI chapter heading "Acceptance, not Appetite" in the publisher's preview matches it.
- Coverage is low. The long closing chapter "What is Design?" was not available and may qualify or extend these ideas.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/masters/timeless-principles.md` and `references/masters/master-lenses.md` already cite Hara for emptiness and "this will do". This file adds Re-Design, Haptic and Senseware, Exformation and the empty-vessel theory of communication. It sits close to Rams's "less, but better", but Hara rejects equating his aesthetic with simplicity. Emptiness is about receptivity, not reduction.
- `references/books/krug-dont-make-me-think.md` and `references/books/design-of-everyday-things.md` say interfaces should be self-evident and signified. That conflicts with "leave room for interpretation". Trust Krug and Norman for interaction and Hara for brand, marketing and physical or sensory expression.
- `references/masters/timeless-principles.md` §tensions (minimal vs rich; Alexander's "life" from texture) is closer to Hara's Haptic and Senseware chapters than to Swiss minimalism. Hara's restraint is full of texture and material.
- `references/books/everything-is-a-remix.md` and the "tip of Asia" chapter agree that refinement of absorbed influences is a form of originality.
