# "Most advanced, yet acceptable" (MAYA) — Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen (British Journal of Psychology 2003)

Use this when: deciding how far a redesign, a new product category or a brand surface may depart from what people expect; when someone cites "MAYA" or Loewy to justify either boldness or timidity; when you plan an aesthetic preference test and need to measure typicality and novelty separately.

Basis note: the 2003 article is closed access (OpenAlex: closed, no repository full text). These notes rest on (1) the authors' published abstract (PubMed), (2) Berghman & Hekkert's open-access DRS 2016 paper "The beauty of balance", which re-tests MAYA inside Hekkert's later Unified Model of Aesthetics (read in full), and (3) a 2017 TU Delft interview with Hekkert. ## The big idea (3 sentences)
Typical designs are liked because they are easy to recognise and categorise, and novel designs are liked because they offer something new, but typicality and novelty are strongly negatively correlated, so each hides the other's positive effect. In three studies with consumer products, neither variable correlated with aesthetic preference on its own, yet both became strong, roughly equal predictors once the other was partialled out. People prefer the most novel design that does not cost typicality: Raymond Loewy's "most advanced, yet acceptable" turned into a testable, two-variable model.

## Core concepts
- **Typicality** — how good an example of its category an object is ("goodness of example"). Study 3 also used an objective version, *central tendency*: an exemplar's average similarity to all other category members. Both behaved the same.
- **Novelty** — how original or new the design looks to the viewer. Rated separately from typicality, not as its opposite.
- **Mutual suppression** — because typicality and novelty pull against each other, simple correlations with preference were not significant; partial correlations were highly significant. This is the statistical core of MAYA and the reason single-variable studies of "familiarity" or "originality" give contradictory answers.
- **Joint and equal effect** — across categories (sanders, telephones and teakettles, per the secondary summary), the two predictors contributed about equally, with the balance tilting by category.
- **Expertise did not change the mix** — Study 2 predicted that design experts would weigh novelty more; the abstract reports expertise did not affect the relative contribution. (The secondary summary notes this was the hypothesis; the abstract is the authority on the result.)
- **Optimal combination** — preferred products are those "with an optimal combination of both aspects": not maximal novelty, not maximal typicality.
- **Unified Model of Aesthetics (UMA)** — Hekkert's later framework (2006, 2014) places MAYA as the cognitive level of one underlying tension between a need for *safety* and a need for *accomplishment*. The perceptual level is unity-in-variety; the social level is connectedness-yet-autonomy (belonging while standing out).
- **Evolutionary framing** — aesthetic pleasure is treated as a by-product of sense-making: fluent processing that identifies prospects for safety and for exploration feels good. This is a theory, not something the studies test.
- **Culture and history move the anchor** — what counts as typical depends on what a person has seen; Hekkert argues the principle is universal while its content varies by culture, time and place.

## What the 2016 re-test found (Berghman & Hekkert, read in full)
- 300 US Mechanical Turk respondents rated 20 products (bicycles, sunglasses, dining tables, espresso makers, table lamps) on 7-point items for appreciation, unity, variety, typicality, novelty, connectedness and autonomy; cross-classified multilevel models with random intercepts for people and products.
- Typicality and novelty correlated about −0.70. Typicality's raw correlation with appreciation was slightly *negative* (−0.15); controlling for novelty, it turned positive (b ≈ 0.14), the same suppression pattern as 2003. Novelty had the larger effect (b ≈ 0.43). Typicality plus novelty explained about 12% of variance; unity plus variety explained about 34%.
- In the combined model (42% explained), typicality dropped to non-significance: about half its effect was carried by perceived unity and half by social connectedness. Unity (0.36) and variety (0.33) stayed the strongest predictors; novelty (0.10) stayed significant.
- Authors' reading: people like typical products mostly because they look coherent and socially safe; novelty adds pleasure in its own right. For designers, the perceptual balance of unity and variety is the most controllable lever.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a redesign is meant to feel fresh, change surface qualities (type, colour, imagery, motion, detailing) before you change the category's recognisable structure (nav position, layout regions, control shapes), because novelty pays only while the object still reads as a good example of its kind.
- When stakeholders argue "bold vs safe", reframe it as two dials, not one: measure typicality and novelty separately in a preference test (two items each), then look at both partial effects. A single "how innovative is this?" score will mislead you because of suppression.
- When you test concepts, include the current or category-typical design as an anchor and expect novel concepts to win only if they keep typicality ratings close to that anchor; a concept that is much more novel but much less typical is the MAYA failure case.
- When a surface is functional (forms, checkout, settings, navigation), keep typicality high and spend novelty on detail and delight, because typicality there also carries learnability (see Jakob's Law in `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md`), which MAYA does not even measure.
- When a surface is expressive (brand, campaign, onboarding moments), push novelty further, but keep at least one strong category cue so people can still classify what they see in a glance.
- When a product creates a new category (AI-native tools, new hardware), borrow typicality from the nearest familiar category (chat, document, timeline) and add novelty gradually across releases, because the "acceptable" line moves with exposure.
- When you hear "make it unified and rich at the same time", take it literally: the 2016 data say perceived unity and variety together predicted beauty far better than typicality and novelty did. Fix coherence (alignment, consistent radii, palette discipline) before debating originality.
- When targeting a specific audience, research what is typical *for them* (their tools, their competitors, their culture), because typicality is relative to experience, not to the designer's taste.
- When you cite MAYA in a critique, cite it as a direction ("stay recognisable, then differ"), never as proof that a specific design will convert; it predicts liking of product images, not behaviour.

## Evidence & caveats
- Strengths: the suppression finding is a real statistical insight and has held across the original three studies, the objective central-tendency measure, and an independent online sample in 2016. Several later studies by Hekkert's group (Thurgood et al. 2014; Blijlevens et al. 2014; not read here) report compatible results.
- The original studies used images of physical consumer products (sanders, telephones, kettles), not screens; generalisation to UI is by analogy. Tuch et al. 2012 (`tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md`) is the closest screen evidence and found typicality effects on first impressions of websites.
- Correlational designs: products were rated, not manipulated, so the paper shows associations between perceived typicality, novelty and liking, not that changing one causes the other to change liking.
- Outcomes are self-reported aesthetic preference for pictures. No purchase, usage, conversion or long-term satisfaction data.
- The 2016 re-test weakens the special status of typicality: once unity and social connectedness are controlled, typicality adds nothing. MAYA may partly be "coherent and socially safe" in disguise.
- Effect sizes and sample sizes of the 2003 studies were not available to us; treat the "equal contribution" claim as the authors' summary.
- The evolutionary story (safety vs accomplishment) is a plausible framing, not tested here. Do not present it as established science.
- Loewy's original MAYA was a designer's maxim from his memoir; the paper supports a version of it, not his broader marketing claims.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md` tags MAYA as [moderate]. Agreed, with the caveat above that typicality's effect may run through unity and social fit.
- `tuch-visual-complexity-prototypicality.md` found low complexity plus high typicality best for websites, with atypical pages scoring poorly whatever their complexity. That reads as "typicality first" more than MAYA's "balance". Trust Tuch for functional screens and first impressions; trust MAYA for product and brand aesthetics where novelty is part of value.
- Berlyne-style "moderate arousal is best" (sometimes repeated in design writing) is a cousin of MAYA. Tuch found no inverted U for complexity on websites; MAYA does not claim an inverted U either, but an optimum of two correlated variables. Do not merge the two ideas.
- `references/masters/taste.md` and the expressive-design references push distinctiveness. MAYA does not oppose them; it says distinctiveness must stay within what the audience can still classify.
