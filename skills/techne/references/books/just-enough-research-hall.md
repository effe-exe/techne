# Just Enough Research — Erika Hall

Use this when: a team wants to "do some research" but hasn't said what decision it should inform; someone proposes a survey to settle a design argument or to stand in for talking to users; stakeholders dismiss qualitative findings ("only 8 users") or ignore research that contradicts them; you need to plan a small, fast study that fits a deadline.

Basis: the book (A Book Apart 2013; 2nd ed. 2019; revised Mule Books edition 2024) was not read in full. These notes come from Hall's own free material, read in full: two official excerpts, chapter 9 "Surveys" (2024 edition, published as a free sample by her publisher Mule Books, ending partway through the sampling section) and chapter 5 "Interviewing Humans" (1st edition, published as an excerpt on A List Apart, 2013). Also read are her Mule Design essays that restate the book's argument: "You Can't Do Enough", "You Need More Enough" (her summary of what the 2024 edition covers), "The 9 Rules of Design Research", "A Design Research Framework", "Quick Tips for Picking Design Research Activities", "Design Research Maturity in Five Questions", "Brainstorm Questions" and "Summary Judgment". Covered: the philosophy, the order of operations, interviewing, surveys and research politics. Not covered: the chapters on organizational, competitive and evaluative research (usability testing, heuristics), analysis and models (personas, mental models, affinity diagrams), quantitative research and A/B testing, and the full survey chapter.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Design research is "asking and answering questions in a systematic way" so that a team can make more informed decisions within its real constraints. "Just enough" means as much research as the decision and the size of the knowledge gap require: neither an academic standard nor an excuse for sloppy work. The hard part is rarely the method. It is clarifying goals, admitting what you don't know, agreeing on questions before choosing activities, and making sure those with power actually use the findings, since more data rarely changes a mind committed to an answer.

## Core concepts
- **Design research, not just user research** — the questions span the organization (how decisions get made, capabilities, politics), the competition and context as well as users. A narrow focus on "the user" can hide the wider system and the ethics of the business.
- **Enough is set by the decision** — like trip planning: a solo weekend away needs less than a 100-person reunion. The standard of quality is whether research improves decisions in time, not the polish of the report.
- **Order of operations** — goals, then the decisions and their deadlines, then the knowledge gaps, then prioritised questions, then which questions merit research, then what "enough" data means, then the plan, then the work, then collaborative analysis, then applying the insights. In a working team, steps 1–6 take an hour or two.
- **Research question ≠ interview question** — the research question is what you want to know. Asking it directly ("would you use X?") is often the worst way to learn, because people are good at making up answers.
- **Question categories** — generative or exploratory, descriptive, evaluative and causal. Each needs different activities, and a method that is wrong for the category produces data nobody can use. Describe before you measure.
- **Qual vs quant** — to understand something, use qualitative methods (observation, interviews, diaries). To measure something, use genuinely quantitative methods designed to give generalisable results. You can't measure what you don't yet understand.
- **Ask first, prototype later** — a prototype is an answer, and testing one only refines an idea; it can't tell you whether it solves the right problem. Polish is easily mistaken for quality.
- **Brainstorm questions, not ideas** — idea brainstorming feels collaborative but tends towards groupthink and competition. Listing questions and plotting them by how little you know against how critical the answer is builds shared understanding and makes "I don't know" safe to say.
- **There is always enough time and money** — objections about time and budget usually cover fear of being proven wrong. Working back from the decision date, you can always learn something (read existing studies, observe in public, test a competitor's product).
- **Data doesn't change minds** — evidence that threatens a decision-maker's beliefs or position gets rejected. Researchers should study their own organization's decision-making as carefully as they study users.
- **Interviewing** — the skill is mainly keeping quiet. Prepare a guide (goal, background facts, warm-ups, topics) and follow three acts (introduction and consent, body, wrap-up with "anything else?"). Ask open questions, allow silence, probe vague answers, treat the guide as a checklist, never talk about yourself, write down people's exact words, and listen after the recorder stops.
- **Surveys are the most dangerous method** — easy to make, easy to send and easy to count, which makes their results feel true. A bad survey gives no sign that it is bad: no failed test, no awkward silence, only bad data at scale. Most surveys mix qualitative and quantitative questions and inherit the pitfalls of both. A survey is a model of a population only when the sampling frame and sample are representative. And even then it measures how people would answer, not what is true.
- **Bias buddies** — no one can see their own biases. Interpret findings together against the goals and questions agreed beforehand, in a team where it is safe to point out each other's biases.
- **Integration over repositories** — research that doesn't reach decisions disappears. A research repository can't replace people talking to each other. Plan how insights will be used before planning the study.
- **Specific quotes over summaries** — the value of qualitative material is in specific, well-chosen quotes. A frequent theme isn't necessarily a relevant one, so beware generic summaries, AI-generated ones included.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- Before agreeing to any research request, write one line each for: the goal, the decision it informs, who makes that decision, the deadline, and what you will do differently for each likely answer. If there is no decision, there is no study. Run a one-hour "what do we already know, and how do we know it?" session first; often that is enough.
- Run a 30–60 minute question-storm with the team and at least one decision-maker. Ask senior people to model "I don't know". Plot questions by uncertainty against risk and investigate only the top-right quadrant. Keep the list as a living backlog.
- Classify each research question (generative, descriptive, evaluative, causal) before choosing a method, and check the fit. Analytics can't explain why; interviews can't estimate percentages; a usability test can't prove cause; only a controlled experiment can (`references/data-driven/experimentation-playbook.md`).
- Discuss sample size and qualitative saturation with stakeholders before fieldwork, not when they wave the results away with "only 8 users". Agree in advance what level of evidence will count as enough for this decision (`references/process/research-methods-selector.md` §4–5).
- When someone proposes a survey, ask: do we already know the right questions and answer options from qualitative work? Can we reach a representative sample frame? What would we do differently with the result? If any answer is no, do interviews or observation first. Never use a survey as a fallback when you're "not allowed" to talk to users. Test a competitor's product with recruited non-customers instead.
- Never let a survey statistic become a design rationale on its own ("75% said they love autoplay"). Check it against behavioural data or a secondary source before it spreads into decision-makers' heads.
- For interviews, write a guide with the study goal, context questions, two warm-ups and open prompts ("Walk me through the last time you…", "Tell me more about that"). Use it as a checklist, not a script. Count your own talk time afterwards; if you spoke more than about 20% of the time, fix it next round. Write down people's exact words for later interface copy.
- Don't jump to prototype testing to answer "should we build this?" Test prototypes only when the problem is known and you are choosing between solutions. For "is this the right problem?", use observation and interviews.
- If research keeps being ignored, study how decisions are actually made in your organisation: who decides, on what kind of evidence, under what incentives. Involve decision-makers in forming the questions and in analysis rather than handing them reports. People listen to findings they helped shape.
- Analyse together, against the original goals and questions, with someone assigned to challenge interpretations. Share specific quotes and observed behaviour, not just summaries or "themes by frequency".
- When time is very short, scale the method down, not the thinking: read existing public research, do guerrilla observation, run five-minute intercepts, or usability-test a competitor. Never skip the goal and question steps; Hall says those are where quality comes from.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a practitioner's book of opinion and craft from a design consultancy founder, not an empirical study. Its claims about organisations (fear of questions, research ignored for political reasons) come from experience and observation. They are plausible and widely echoed by practitioners, but not measured.
- Several claims align with research: group idea brainstorming underperforms individuals working alone (the nominal-group findings, e.g. Diehl & Stroebe 1987; context, not cited by Hall in these sources); people confabulate reasons and predictions about their own behaviour; and motivated reasoning makes evidence that contradicts existing beliefs easy to reject. These support her advice.
- "Surveys are the most dangerous method" is a deliberate overstatement. Well-designed surveys with probability or well-understood samples (Pew, validated scales such as SUS) are reliable for measuring prevalence and attitudes. Her point is about cheap, unscreened, poorly worded product surveys. Combine her caution with the survey practice in `references/books/quantifying-the-user-experience.md` and `references/process/research-methods-selector.md` §6.
- The "just enough" label is often misused to justify thin research. Hall herself rejects that reading: "enough" is set by the decision risk, and some decisions need a lot.
- The interview excerpt is from the 2013 edition and suggests turning clusters into personas "for the life of the product". Treat personas as provisional models to revisit, not permanent truth.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/books/mom-test.md` agrees on avoiding hypotheticals and leading questions and on learning from past behaviour. Fitzpatrick favours casual, unscheduled conversations without a formal "interview". Hall prepares a guide, gets consent and records. Use Hall's structure for planned studies and Mom Test habits for everyday customer conversations.
- `references/books/continuous-discovery-habits-torres.md` asks for weekly interviews as a standing habit. Hall asks for research to be pulled by specific decisions and questions. They are compatible: keep Torres's cadence, but tie each round to the current question backlog.
- `references/books/lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md` and `references/books/ulwick-jobs-to-be-done-theory-to-practice.md` favour scoring interviews and large importance-and-satisfaction surveys. Hall is sceptical of turning qualitative material into numbers and of survey-first research. Use ODI-style surveys only after qualitative work has produced the outcome statements, and with proper sampling.
- `references/process/research-methods-selector.md` is the method-selection and sample-size reference; this file is the "why and when" layer above it. Where the selector lists surveys as a default for sizing a need, add Hall's preconditions (known questions, a representative frame, a decision attached).
