# Influence (New and Expanded ed.) — Robert B. Cialdini

Use this when: you are writing persuasive product copy (pricing pages, sign-up prompts, conservation or safety nudges, review displays) and want to know which influence lever applies; someone proposes urgency, social proof or authority badges and you need to separate the honest use from the manipulative one; a message about a "widespread problem" might accidentally normalize the behavior you want to stop.

Basis: the book (Harper Business, 2021; earlier editions 1984–2009) was not read. These notes rest on Cialdini's own legally free material: the **official principles page** on his company's site, Influence at Work, written in his voice ("My own research has identified seven of these shortcuts…"; read in full), which summarizes the six original principles with his preferred studies; his **2007 testimony to the US House Science Committee** on social norms and energy conservation (public record, read in full), which covers his normative-messaging research (Petrified Forest, door hangers, hotel towels); and the **official announcement and book descriptions** of the new edition (a seventh principle, Unity; new research; online applications; about 220 extra pages). Unity is covered only by name and by brief author quotes in a 2021 ASU news item (it gets a 73-page chapter in the book). Coverage is medium for the six classic principles and the social-norms work. It is low for Unity, the contrast principle, the "click-whirr" automatic-response framing, the defense sections and the new online examples.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Overloaded people decide with mental shortcuts, and seven of them are near-universal triggers for saying yes: reciprocity, scarcity, authority, commitment/consistency, liking, social proof and (new in 2021) unity. Each shortcut is usually a sensible rule (repay favors, follow experts, do what similar others do), which is why small, often costless changes in how a request is framed can produce large shifts in compliance. The same levers can be used ethically, by pointing to true and relevant facts, or exploited by faking them, so a persuader should use them only honestly and a consumer should learn to spot the fakes.

## Core concepts
- **Shortcuts under overload** — the less time and attention people have, the more they rely on a single cue instead of weighing all the information.
- **Reciprocity** — people feel obliged to return what they received first. The restaurant-mint studies he cites: one mint ≈ +3% tips, two mints ≈ +14%, one mint plus a personal "for you nice people" second mint ≈ +23%. His lesson is to give first, personally and unexpectedly. How you give matters more than what you give.
- **Scarcity** — people want more of what is less available. His example is the Concorde's last flights selling out once retirement was announced. He also says to point out what is unique and what people stand to lose.
- **Authority** — people follow credible experts. Displaying physiotherapists' diplomas raised exercise compliance. Receptionists who introduced agents' experience ("Sandra, 15 years letting in this area") lifted appointments 20% and contracts 15%. Signal credibility before the request, ideally through a third party.
- **Commitment & consistency** — people act in line with prior commitments. The Drive Safely study: a small window card ten days earlier led to four times as many people agreeing to a big lawn sign. Commitments that are voluntary, active, public and written are strongest. Having patients write their own appointment card cut no-shows 18%.
- **Liking** — similarity, compliments and cooperation toward shared goals make us agree. In negotiation studies among MBA students, swapping personal information and finding a similarity first raised agreement from about 55% to 90%, with deals about 18% more valuable.
- **Social proof** — under uncertainty people copy others, especially similar others. Hotel towels: a "most guests reuse" card beat the environmental appeal, and "most guests *in this room*" did best. His page gives +26% and +33%, the testimony says +34% on average, and the JCR paper reports 35.1% → 44.1%.
- **Norm backfire** (testimony) — messages lamenting how common a bad behavior is ("14 tons of wood stolen every year") send the hidden message "everyone does it". At the Petrified Forest, a sign showing several thieves more than doubled theft, while a sign showing a lone thief nearly halved it.
- **Descriptive vs injunctive norms** — what most people do vs what most people approve. Align them. Publicize the true majority behavior when it is good, and present bad behavior as a small minority's.
- **Normative influence is underdetected** — in Nolan et al. (door hangers, San Diego), residents rated neighbors' behavior as the least motivating reason. Yet it was the only message that cut energy use (about 2 kWh/day). People's own predictions of what will persuade them are unreliable.
- **Unity** (2021) — influence from shared identity, "us". Cialdini says research shows it can move people from hesitant or resistant to compliant. The free sources give no detail. In *Pre-Suasion* (2016, not read here) it is described as shared identity built by being together and acting together. Treat these details as unverified for this file.
- **Ethics** — he repeatedly frames use as ethical when the lever points to something true (real expertise, real scarcity, real majorities). The book adds defenses against manipulation.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- **Reciprocity:** give real value before asking (a working free tier, a useful template, an answer) and make the gift feel personal (based on the user's data, unexpected, from a named person). Never disguise an ask as a gift, such as a "free gift" that starts a paid trial (see the dark-pattern files).
- **Scarcity:** state real limits only: actual stock, real deadlines, true capacity. Tell users what they'd specifically lose by waiting ("price rises on 1 March for new sign-ups") rather than generic urgency. Fake timers and fake low-stock badges are banned (FTC and EDPB guidance in `references/psychology/behavior-law-consent-cancellation.md`).
- **Authority:** show verifiable credentials where the decision happens (a named reviewer, a certification with a link, years of expertise). Third-party introductions beat self-praise. In marketplaces and support, have the system introduce the expert ("Ana, 12 years in tax returns, will review this").
- **Commitment:** let users make small, active, self-chosen commitments (pick their own goal, type their own plan, choose a reminder time), then show progress against them. Ask users to enter key details themselves (an appointment, a pledge) rather than pre-filling them, when remembering matters. Don't chain small yeses into an unwanted purchase.
- **Liking:** build rapport through real similarity and cooperation (shared context, "teams like yours", a human support voice, a collaborative tone). Fake personas and invented similarity are manipulation.
- **Social proof:** use honest, specific and similar reference groups: "most teams your size enable 2FA", "people in this room…". Similarity and specificity matter more than raw totals. Show real counts and recent activity only.
- **Never advertise how common the bad behavior is.** Don't write "70% of users never finish setup" to motivate finishing. State the desirable majority if true ("Most new users finish setup in under 5 minutes"), or present the problem as the exception.
- When a descriptive norm could push above-average users toward the average (the "boomerang" in the energy studies by his coauthors), pair it with approval for the good behavior (a smiley, "great job"), or show it only to people below the norm.
- Don't trust user surveys about what will persuade them. Cialdini's door-hanger study shows people deny being moved by others. Test messages in live experiments.
- **Unity:** speak to a shared identity that really exists (your community of practitioners, "built with our users", co-creation). Avoid manufactured tribalism and us-versus-them shaming.
- Pick one lever per moment. Many stacked persuasion cues (badges + timer + "12 people viewing") read as pressure and erode trust. Test them, and measure regret metrics (refunds, cancellations, complaints), not just conversion.

## Evidence & caveats
- The principles are robust as categories of social influence, but many classic demonstrations are small, old or field studies without preregistration. Effect sizes on the official page are presented with no confidence intervals.
- **Hotel towels:** the headline social-proof study has had mixed replications. Bohner & Schlüter (2014, Germany) didn't replicate the norm effect, and a Bayesian meta-analysis (Scheibehenne, Jamil & Wagenmakers 2016) found a small effect. Cialdini's own sources report different percentages for the same work (+26%/+33% on the website, +34% average in the testimony, 35.1% → 44.1% in the paper). Treat the magnitude as uncertain.
- **Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face:** meta-analyses show small, variable average effects. Don't expect "four times" as a typical lift.
- **Mints and tips:** a small restaurant field study (Strohmetz et al. 2002). Scarcity's Concorde example is an anecdote, not a test.
- **Conflict of interest:** in 2007 Cialdini disclosed a consulting and equity relationship with Positive Energy (later Opower), which sends neighbor-comparison energy reports. Opower's large randomized trials (Allcott 2011, run by others) did confirm modest savings of about 2%, but that disclosure is worth knowing when reading his norm claims.
- Cialdini isn't part of the priming or ego-depletion replication controversies, but popular summaries of *Pre-Suasion* lean on priming-style effects. Treat "attention priming before the message" claims cautiously until checked against preregistered work.
- Unity and other 2021 additions were not read. Don't attribute specific Unity studies to the book from this file.
