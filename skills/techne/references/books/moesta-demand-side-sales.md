# Demand-Side Sales 101 — Bob Moesta

Use this when: sign-ups, trials or demos stall even though people say they like the product; you are designing onboarding, a pricing page, a trial-to-paid flow or a sales or demo sequence and need to know where buyers actually are in their decision; you want to run switch interviews to learn why people bought, left or never started.

Basis: the book (Lioncrest, 2020, with Greg Engle, foreword by Jason Fried) was not read. These notes come from Moesta's own free material, all read in full: the official Author Hour interview about the book from his publisher Scribe (full transcript); his 2020 How to Web Live conversation about the book (full transcript), where he walks through the buying timeline as a sales process, the "three demos" and the big hire / little hire; the Re-Wired Group's own JTBD guides on jobstobedone.org (four forces, struggling moment, timeline, switch interview, "What is JTBD"), written by Moesta and Chris Spiek; the JTBD Radio episode with Clay Christensen; the Brand Lab podcast transcript with the mattress-interview story; and his 2016 Intercom podcast transcript (big and little hire, zombie revenue). Coverage of the book's theory (forces, timeline, struggling moment, demand vs supply side) is good. Its detailed case studies, chapter structure and worksheets are not covered.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Sales has techniques but no theory, and it is taught from the supply side ("I make mattresses; who needs one?") rather than from the demand side ("who can't sleep, and what is making them decide that today is the day?"). Nobody buys at random: each purchase is caused by a struggling moment, follows a timeline from first thought through passive and active looking to deciding, and happens only when the push of the situation and the pull of the new option outweigh the anxiety of the new and the habit of the present. Selling, then, means helping people make progress at the point on their timeline where they actually are, which also makes marketing, sales and customer success one continuous job instead of a set of handoffs.

## Core concepts
- **Supply side vs demand side** — supply side starts from the product and looks for buyers. Demand side starts from the struggle and the progress the person wants, regardless of product. Teams that call themselves "customer-focused" usually see the customer through their product.
- **No random purchases** — "impulse" buys turn out to have long causes. The Costco mattress bought on a whim followed two years of poor sleep and workarounds (sleeping pills, the recliner). Everything has a cause, even if we haven't found it yet.
- **Struggling moment** — the point where the status quo becomes unacceptable. It has three possible outcomes: switch, keep searching, or do nothing (nonconsumption). Habitual repurchasers have no struggle and teach you little.
- **Four forces of progress** — push of the situation and pull of the new solution drive change. Anxiety of the new solution and habit of the present hold it back. A switch happens only when push + pull > anxiety + habit. Most teams over-invest in pull (features, benefits) and ignore anxiety and habit. More features can increase anxiety.
- **Timeline** — first thought → passive looking → event 1 → active looking → event 2 → deciding → consuming. The first thought is usually much earlier than people report (home buyers' real first thought was between Thanksgiving and New Year, while the industry stopped advertising in November). In active looking people "shape the job" through trade-offs, which is why asking for features before they have shopped is unreliable.
- **Timeline as sales process** — create a first thought (ask a question, tell a story, give a new metric, state the obvious). In passive looking help them frame the problem and learn the language. In active looking show possibilities. In deciding connect the dots, show constraints and trade-offs, and pick a starting point. Then set expectations and make the first progress felt. Then build habits, which bring new struggling moments.
- **Two moments of truth** — the first is when the forces tip and people buy, which is "consumption in the mind". The second is actual use, where satisfaction is judged, often on different criteria than the purchase. The gap between imagined and actual use determines satisfaction.
- **Big hire and little hire** — the big hire is buying or signing up; the little hire is each time the product is used. Real value is created in the little hire. Trial comes from the big hire, repeat use only from the little one. B2B adds buyer and user, with different jobs; interview both and look for conflicts between them.
- **Context creates value, contrast creates meaning** — "do you like steak or pizza?" can't be answered without the occasion. Pizza fits the "extend the picnic with four people" context and steak doesn't. Averaging across contexts produces a product nobody wants (the thick-or-thin frozen pizza). The relevant segment is when and where, not just who.
- **Switch interview** — a forensic reconstruction of a recent switch, built on interrogation techniques. Recruit people who switched in the last 30–90 days, including near-switchers. Frame the interview like a documentary, find the first thought, walk events rather than opinions, listen for the forces, and capture their exact words. Techniques: play dumb, deliberately connect the wrong dots, interview in pairs, let people change their story. Patterns show up within 5–8 interviews per job. Each runs 45–90 minutes.
- **Hypothesis-building, not hypothesis-verifying** — delay judgment and extract stories first. The job is to find causes, not to confirm a pitch.
- **Zombie revenue** — people who pay but don't use the product. Either the job is done, they don't know what to do next, or the product does a different job than the company assumes (photo storage hired as "the box in the closet"). Ask "why haven't you quit?" rather than "why did you stop using it?"

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When conversion stalls, audit the bottom of the forces before the top. List every anxiety (cost of a wrong choice, data lock-in, looking foolish, setup effort) and every habit (existing tool, muscle memory, sunk data). Design specifically against each one: guarantees, import tools, reversible trials, "keep your old workflow" modes, proof from similar people. Moesta and Spiek's OpenTable example won by removing the friction and anxiety of booking, not by adding appeal to dining out.
- Before adding a feature "to win deals", test whether it adds anxiety (more to learn, more to configure, more to go wrong). Treat new options as a cost to the forces, not a free pull.
- Map your funnel to the buyer's timeline, not your sales stages. Give different content and different calls to action to passive lookers (framing, language, "is this problem big enough?"), active lookers (possibilities, comparisons) and deciders (trade-offs, constraints, a starting point, pricing clarity). Asking "where are you in your decision?" beats "which plan do you want?"
- Split one demo, tour or onboarding into versions for each stage. Moesta reports that splitting a single demo into passive, active and deciding versions doubled sales and halved the cycle at a client (Autobooks). Treat that as a hypothesis to A/B test, not a benchmark.
- Use the customer's own struggle words in headlines and empty states ("I'm falling behind", "I just got fed up"). Don't use your feature vocabulary. Losing ground ("falling behind") and getting ahead are different energies and need different copy.
- Design the little hire as carefully as the big hire. The promise made at sign-up sets the expectations that the first sessions must meet. Measure time to first real progress, not just activation events (see `references/data-driven/conversion-onboarding-activation-retention.md`).
- For inactive paying users, interview with "what's kept you from cancelling?" Offer an honest "park my account / archive mode" when the job is done, rather than reactivation nags, which build resentment. Never make leaving hard (see `references/psychology/behavior-law-consent-cancellation.md`).
- Interview recent switchers, both to you and away from you, within 90 days. Start with a competitor's customers if you can't keep your own product out of the conversation. Don't ask "why did you buy?" Ask what was going on before that, until you reach the first thought.
- For B2B, run separate interviews with buyers (big hire) and users (little hire). Design the admin and procurement experience as a product too, and resolve conflicts between the two jobs explicitly.
- Expect each innovation to solve one set of struggling moments and create new ones. After launch, re-interview to find the new struggles that habit-building has exposed.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a practitioner framework from a consultant. Its evidence is client stories (condo sales up 25% while the industry fell 49%; a 17% rise in home sales; "double sales, half the cycle"), which are self-reported and uncontrolled. None of these results come from published studies.
- The forces model is a qualitative, descriptive model, not a fitted or validated scale. "Push + pull > anxiety + habit" can't be measured as an inequality. It is a useful lens for interviews and audits. Where it touches measurable things it agrees with better evidence: status quo bias and loss aversion (habit and anxiety; see `references/books/thinking-fast-and-slow-kahneman.md`, with the caution that some loss-aversion magnitudes are debated) and friction effects in behavior design (`references/books/fogg-behavior-model.md`).
- The interviewing approach is said to draw on criminal and intelligence interrogation. The framing is rhetorical, but the core practice of walking concrete past events and avoiding opinions and hypotheticals is consistent with recall research and with `references/books/mom-test.md`. Memory of purchases is reconstructive, so even switch interviews yield stories, not facts. Triangulate with behavioral data.
- "Patterns in 5–8 interviews" and "you can see an entire market in 10 interviews" are saturation claims for qualitative coding. They can't size a segment or estimate how common a force is. Use a survey or analytics for that.
- Several examples (the milkshake, the condo dining-room table) are shared with Christensen and appear in slightly different versions across tellings. Treat details as illustrative.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/process/process-playbook.md` and `references/process/research-methods-selector.md` already summarize the four forces and switch interviews (10–15 switchers). That's consistent. This file adds the timeline-as-funnel idea, big hire and little hire, and zombie revenue. The 5–8 per job here versus 10–15 there reflects per-job versus per-study counts.
- `references/books/christensen-competing-against-luck.md` is the theory side; this is the method and sales side. Christensen emphasizes integrating the organization around a job. Moesta emphasizes the buyer's timeline and forces.
- `references/books/influence-cialdini.md` and `references/psychology/behavior-persuasion-toolkit.md` add persuasion levers (social proof, scarcity). Moesta's approach is to reduce anxiety and help people decide, not to apply pressure. Fake urgency to "create a first thought" fails the techne ethics gate (`references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`), and his own critique of end-of-quarter discounts says they devalue the product.
- `references/data-driven/experimentation-playbook.md`: forces audits produce hypotheses. Ship changes that reduce anxiety and habit as controlled experiments with guardrails.
