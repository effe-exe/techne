# Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? — Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd

Use this when: someone wants to cut a catalog, plan grid, menu, template gallery or settings list "because of the jam study"; you are deciding whether to add options, a recommended default or categories to a choice screen; you need to explain why a famous behavioral finding should not be copied as a rule.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Pooling 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) on whether larger assortments reduce the motivation to choose or satisfaction with the choice, the authors find a mean effect of essentially zero (d = 0.02, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.12). The effects vary more than chance alone would explain, but much of that spread comes from a handful of studies with large effects in both directions, and no measured moderator was a sufficient condition for overload. Choice overload is therefore a conditional, fragile effect, not a law, and design decisions should rest on the structure of the choice and the chooser's preferences rather than on the raw count of options.

## Core concepts
- **Choice overload hypothesis** — the claim that more options can reduce choosing, commitment or satisfaction. It contradicts standard choice theory (the regularity axiom: adding options can't make you worse off), which is why it drew so much attention.
- **The canonical studies** — Iyengar & Lepper 2000: a tasting table with 24 jams drew more shoppers than one with 6, but about 3% vs 30% bought; 30 vs 6 chocolates gave lower satisfaction and fewer people taking chocolate as payment (12% vs 48%). These are the effects the meta-analysis tests.
- **Failed direct replications** — Scheibehenne's own replication of the jam study in a German upscale supermarket found nothing (n = 504, d = 0.02, the heaviest-weighted point in the analysis); replications with chocolates and jelly beans also failed.
- **Mean effect near zero** — random-effects model across all 63 data points. Trimming the 20% most extreme studies leaves d = 0.001 and drops heterogeneity from I² = 68% to 22%: a few outliers in both directions create most of the variance.
- **"More is better" moderators that held up in the meta-regression** —
  - *consumption quantity* as the outcome (people eat more from a larger, organized assortment; driven by Kahn & Wansink 2004);
  - *prior preferences or expertise* (people who know what they want benefit from more options; Chernev 2003, Mogilner et al. 2008).
- **Publication bias and a decline effect** — journal articles report larger overload effects than unpublished work, and newer studies find less overload (the authors call it a "Prometheus effect": surprising findings publish first, follow-ups shrink them).
- **What did not moderate** — real vs hypothetical choice, satisfaction vs choice as the outcome, US vs non-US samples, size of the large set (no linear or inverted-U relationship; quadratic fit R² = 0.02).
- **Candidate moderators from single studies (untested meta-analytically)** —
  - *assortment structure*: no categories, hard trade-offs between non-comparable features, many attributes per option (information overload), time pressure;
  - *decision strategy*: evaluating relative "best in set" vs absolute "is this good enough", having to justify the choice;
  - *perception*: whether options seem uniformly high quality.
- **Three different "satisfactions"** — with the chosen item, with the decision process, and with the whole experience. Larger sets reliably feel more difficult and more enjoyable at the same time, so a single satisfaction question mixes these.
- **Not choosing isn't one thing** — it can be procrastination, a status-quo preference, an effort/benefit trade-off, or a sensible deferral because a large set signals better options may come later. Short lab tasks can't tell these apart.
- **Heuristics protect people** — people facing many options usually satisfice, eliminate by aspects, take a default or build a small consideration set, so they rarely overload themselves (Jacoby 1984).

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a stakeholder proposes cutting options "because of the jam study", reply that the pooled effect is about zero and ask for the mechanism in this context (hard comparisons, unclear preferences, time pressure). Without one, test the cut, because removing variety often loses the users who wanted the missing item.
- When users arrive with a clear goal (search queries, returning customers, experts), keep a large assortment and invest in search, filters and sorting, because people with clear preferences choose and are satisfied more often when there is more to choose from.
- When users are novices with no ideal point, give them structure before cutting: categories, a recommended option, a "help me choose" step, because the single studies that found overload point to missing structure and missing preferences, not to the count itself.
- When options differ on many non-comparable attributes (plans, insurance, hardware tiers), align attributes in a comparison table and cut the attribute count first, because the overload findings appear with many attributes per option (Greifeneder et al. 2010) rather than with many options described simply.
- When the flow adds time pressure (countdown, session expiry, a checkout timer), remove the pressure before blaming the number of options, because time pressure was one of the conditions under which more options lowered satisfaction and raised regret.
- When you measure a choice screen, track completion, the chosen item's later retention or refund rate, and perceived difficulty separately, because larger sets change difficulty and enjoyment reliably while the outcome metrics often don't move.
- When a user leaves without choosing from a big set, don't count it as overload by default. Check for return visits and later purchases, because deferral can be a rational response to a rich assortment.
- When you design an experiment on option count, pre-register the outcome and run it at full power on your own traffic, because published effects in this area are inflated by publication bias and shrink over time.
- When you want users to consume or explore more (content, food, music), an organized, larger assortment is the evidence-backed direction, because consumption rose with assortment size in the pooled data.

## Evidence & caveats
- Strong: the average effect across the experiments available by 2009 is close to zero, and that holds after trimming outliers. The largest single study (the German jam replication, n = 504) found nothing.
- Moderate: experts and people with prior preferences benefit from more choice (supported in the meta-regression, p = .013, and by direct manipulations).
- Weak: every other moderator (categories, attributes, time pressure, justification, choose-then-buy order) rests on one or two studies each. The authors list them as research directions, not established conditions.
- The meta-regression explains 56% of the variance; moderate unexplained heterogeneity remains, and a post-hoc split at d = 0.2 hints at two clusters of studies. The authors warn this split is exploratory.
- Effect sizes for seven null results were set to zero because the raw data weren't available. This is conservative toward the null but standard practice.
- Most experiments use low-stakes hedonic items (jam, chocolates, jelly beans, pens) with typical sets of about 6 vs 30. Results may not carry over to consequential choices (retirement plans, health insurance) or to digital catalogs with search.
- Several authors of the meta-analysis also wrote many of the null studies in it, which is worth knowing but doesn't invalidate the pooled result.
- Later work: Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015) meta-analysed more effects and argued overload is reliable when complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty and a must-choose goal combine. That paper is the better guide to when overload happens; this one is the better guide to how often it happens by default (rarely).

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/psychology/behavior-pricing-and-choice.md` §1 summarises this paper correctly but states that categories reduce overload "even meaningless ones" (Mogilner et al. 2008). This paper treats categorization as a single-study moderator, and notes most null studies also lacked categories. Treat "add categories" as a cheap, sensible default, not a proven fix.
- `references/psychology/behavior-pricing-and-choice.md` describes the jam study as 3% vs 30% of those who stopped; this paper describes it as a share of all consumers who saw each display. The direction is the same; quote the numbers loosely and never as a conversion benchmark.
- `references/interaction/platform-conventions.md` and `references/psychology/cognition-motor-control-and-timing.md` link Hick's law to option count. Hick predicts reaction time for familiar choices; this paper is about motivation and satisfaction. Neither supports "fewer options always convert better". Both files already flag that myth, and this paper backs them.
- `references/data-driven/conversion-paywalls-pricing-subscriptions.md` keeps plans few "when they are hard to compare". This paper agrees: the count matters less than comparability.
