# Addiction by Design — Natasha Dow Schüll

Use this when: a team is optimising "time in app", session length or engagement and you need to judge whether the design serves users or captures them; you are designing feeds, autoplay, streaks, loot boxes, rewards or one-tap payments; you need to argue that "frictionless" is not always good and that a stopping point or a decision point protects the user; a stakeholder says only a vulnerable few have problems, so the product is fine.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Modern slot machines are engineered, from maths model and screen to chair, cash system and casino floor, to keep people playing as long as possible ("time on device"). The most devoted players aren't chasing wins but a trance-like "machine zone" in which time, money and the social world drop away. Addiction is therefore produced by the interaction of vulnerable people with technologies designed to be solitary, fast, continuous and uninterrupted, so responsibility can't rest only on the individual.

## Core concepts
- **The machine zone** — the absorbed, self-erasing state players seek. Many players said a jackpot annoyed them because it interrupted play. It is escape from clock time, money value, social interaction and self-management, not thrill.
- **Time on device** — the industry's own metric. Every design layer serves four steps: get you to the machine, keep you there longer, have you play more, and bring you back after the session.
- **The "Costco model"** — profits come from volume: low-rollers, often locals, playing for long periods, valued over their customer lifetime, rather than a few "whales".
- **Smoothing the ride** — computerised odds let designers reduce volatility. Multi-line video slots (up to 100–200 lines) return part of each bet, with the lights and jingles of a win. Schüll cites lab research that people experience these "wins" much like real ones, while they steadily lose money. One designer told her they want players to "recline" on the mathematical model.
- **Event frequency** — up to 1,200 spins an hour. Each event is another chance to reinforce behaviour. In her words, the addictiveness lies in being "solitary, continuous, and really rapid".
- **Ergonomics and ambience** — seats designed not to cut off circulation, buttons and bill acceptors within minimal arm movement, alcoves and nooks for private escape, sound and lighting tuned to avoid fatigue. In casino lore, even the floor plan avoids right angles, because turning makes people stop and decide.
- **Frictionless money** — bill acceptors, player cards and casino ATMs that work around withdrawal limits make paying "almost a non-decision".
- **Player tracking and "behavioural analytics"** — loyalty data are used to tailor marketing and, increasingly, the games themselves. Staff ("luck ambassadors") are sent to players nearing their "pain point", and manufacturers have experimented with changing a game's volatility mid-session while keeping overall payback the same, classed as "marketing".
- **Responsibility** — industry says only 1–2% have a problem, so it's on them. Schüll counters that the industry's whole method treats people as manipulable, so it is "disingenuous" to put all of the responsibility on the person. Some technologies are more liable to addict than others.
- **Regulation** — there's no FDA-equivalent for these machines. Measures debated include reel speed, credit limits, cash-only play, time warnings and voluntary pre-commitment (spend limits set in advance; Massachusetts). Some fixes backfire: slower reels may lengthen sessions.
- **Follow-on (2016)** — online poker players use tracking software to regulate themselves, delegating self-control to software while worrying about becoming "bot-like". Her next book, *Keeping Track*, extends this to self-tracking apps and wearables.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When a metric is time on device, session length or "engagement", ask whose goal it measures. If more time could come from users failing to stop rather than getting value, pair it with outcome and regret measures: tasks done, intended-vs-actual time, "was this time well spent?" ratings, uninstalls, self-reported control.
- When designing continuous content (feeds, autoplay, endless levels), build in natural stopping points: an end of new content, a session summary, autoplay off by default for minors, a clear "you're caught up". Continuity is the core mechanism of the machine zone.
- When rewards come at high frequency, avoid celebrating outcomes that are net losses for the user. A "win" animation for getting back less than you put in (loot boxes, gacha, cashback that costs more than it saves) is the "loss disguised as a win" pattern.
- When removing friction from *paying* (one-tap top-ups, stored credit, auto-reload, in-game currencies), keep a deliberate decision point at meaningful thresholds: show real money, confirm top-ups above a limit, and never hide the running total. Payment smoothness that makes spending a non-decision is how casinos raised play.
- When placing friction, use Schüll's right-angle insight on purpose: add a small pause or explicit choice where users' long-term interests are at stake (continue after an hour, spend beyond a budget, re-enter a habit they asked to break), and remove friction everywhere else.
- When offering self-control tools (time limits, spending caps, focus modes), let users set them *in advance* (pre-commitment), make them binding for the period chosen, and don't make it easier to override them than to set them.
- When using behavioural data to detect a user's "pain point" (about to churn, on a losing streak, frustrated), use it to help them (a break, a refund, a better path), never to time a lure that keeps them playing or spending. If you can detect harm, act as though you have a duty of care.
- When personalising difficulty, odds, prices or rewards in real time, don't adjust them to extend sessions or spending against the user's interest, and disclose any adaptive odds. Calling it "marketing" is how casinos got around the rule against changing odds mid-game.
- When someone argues "only a small fraction of users are harmed", look at revenue concentration. Volume models often earn most from heavy users, so the harmed minority can be the business model. Design and test with the heaviest users in mind.
- When an "anti-addiction" fix is proposed (slower animations, a pop-up warning), test whether it actually shortens harmful sessions. Schüll notes that slowing the reels may *lengthen* play, while time warnings and binding pre-commitment have better support. Measure the outcome, not the gesture.
- When building player or user tracking, ask what you would be obliged to do with what it reveals. If the data can reliably show someone sliding into compulsive use, design the intervention (pause, limit, outreach) together with the tracking.
- When reviewing a design that maximises "flow", ask which flow it is: absorption in a task the user values (good), or a zone that erases the user's sense of time and money (Schüll's machine zone). Clocks, spend totals and session summaries keep people oriented without breaking real flow.
- When building for minors or other vulnerable groups, assume the strictest version of the above. Techne's law and ethics files list the regulatory floor.

## Evidence & caveats
- **Ethnography, not experiment:** the book rests on 15 years of fieldwork with players, designers, executives and regulators. That is strong for *mechanisms and lived experience* and weak for *effect sizes*. The interviews give no numbers on how much each design feature increases play.
- **Cited claims to check before reusing:** that slots earn up to 85% of industry profits (an official's estimate she quotes); that video machines are three to four times more addictive than other forms (Breen's studies, from small clinical samples and debated); and that losses disguised as wins are experienced as wins (lab studies, e.g. Dixon et al. 2010, which measured win-like arousal; cited from memory, not re-read here). The "no right angles" casino-layout claim is industry lore she reports, not a tested result.
- **Slot machines are an extreme case:** real money, continuous and solitary, with mathematically controlled odds. Apps share mechanisms (variable reward, continuity, frictionless payment, tracking), but the link from slot evidence to a social feed or game is an analogy. It is strongest for loot boxes, gacha, betting apps and casino-style mobile games.
- The industry view (stable problem-gambling prevalence; existing standards against deception) is mentioned in the Vox piece. Schüll's position is critical by design: she frames her work as opening a discussion of accountability.
- Coverage limits: none of the book's detailed chapters (near-miss and virtual-reel mapping, casino ambience management, addict narratives, treatment and the industry's "responsible gambling" discourse) were read here.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/books/nudge-thaler-sunstein.md` and `references/books/fogg-behavior-model.md` treat removing friction as making good behaviour easy. Schüll shows the same smoothing producing compulsion. Resolve it by direction: remove friction from actions users would endorse on reflection, and keep or add it where the action extracts value from them (spending, continuing past their own limits). That is the sludge/nudge line applied in reverse.
- `references/psychology/behavior-persuasion-toolkit.md` (Hooked model and its critiques) already cites this book for variable rewards and forbids variable rewards engineered to prolong sessions beyond user intent. This file supplies the mechanism detail behind that rule. The rule stands.
- `references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md` (#17 "Addictive design") and `references/psychology/behavior-law-consent-cancellation.md` (protections for minors) give the remedies and the legal floor. Use Schüll to explain *why* those patterns work.
- `references/data-driven/conversion-onboarding-activation-retention.md` discusses streaks and variable rewards as retention levers with ethical caveats. Where retention depends on users failing to stop, Schüll's analysis says to treat it as harm, not success.
