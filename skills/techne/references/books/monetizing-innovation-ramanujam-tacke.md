# Monetizing Innovation — Madhavan Ramanujam & Georg Tacke

Use this when: you are about to build a new product, feature or plan and nobody has asked customers what they would pay; a roadmap has more features than the team can build and needs a way to prioritise; you are designing plan tiers, bundles or a pricing model (per seat, usage, outcome) and need to decide what goes where.

Basis: the book (Wiley, 2016) was not read in full. Read in full: the publisher's official free excerpts on wiley.com: chapter 1 "How Innovators Leave Billions on the Table: A Tale of Two Cars" (which states the four failure types and all nine rules), the full table of contents and the full index (useful for seeing the book's structure and terms such as leaders, fillers and killers, good/better/best, and the "average response trap"). ## The big idea (3 sentences)
New products usually fail to make money because price is decided last, after the product is designed and built. The fix is to reverse the order: market and price, then design, then build. Price here means perceived value: what customers value and how much they will pay for it. If you have a structured willingness-to-pay (WTP) conversation with customers early, segment them by WTP rather than demographics, and design configurations, bundles and the pricing model around those findings, you can avoid the four ways innovations fail to make money.

## Core concepts
- **Price as perceived value** — "price" is not a number at the end. It is a measure of what customers value and how much. Porsche's Cayenne team tested WTP before the concept car existed, dropped the six-speed manual that engineers loved, and added large cup holders. The Dodge Dart was engineered until "perfect", priced afterwards, and flopped.
- **Design the product around the price** — the usual sequence is design → build → market → price. The book's sequence is market and price → design → build. The authors report that only about 5% of business cases they see contain any WTP data.
- **Four failure types** — *feature shock* (too many features, none standing out, hard to explain, overpriced; Amazon Fire Phone), *minivation* (the right product priced too low; Asus's €299 netbook sold out with demand 900% above supply), *hidden gem* (a potential hit never brought to market because it falls outside the core business; Kodak's 1974 digital camera) and *undead* (a product nobody wants, the wrong answer or an answer to a question nobody asked; Segway).
- **Nine rules** — (1) have the WTP talk early, (2) don't build one-size-fits-all; segment by WTP, (3) configuration and bundling are more science than art, (4) choose the pricing model, because how you charge often matters more than how much, (5) write a pricing strategy that looks a few moves ahead, (6) base the business case on WTP data and link price, value, volume and cost, (7) communicate value clearly, (8) account for irrational behaviour, (9) protect price integrity and use price cuts only as a last resort.
- **The WTP talk** — before building, present the concept and ask: what is an acceptable price, what is an expensive price, what is a prohibitively expensive price? Acceptable is comfortable, "a steal". Expensive is what people will pay without liking it, usually aligned with value. Prohibitive marks the ceiling for now. Plotting the answers shows "price cliffs" and psychological thresholds. Other formats listed in the index include direct questions, purchase-probability questions, most–least (best–worst) questions, build-your-own exercises, purchase simulations and expert-judgement workshops.
- **Prioritise by WTP** — features ranked by what customers will pay for often reverse the team's own priority list. A CEO's exercise found that the features low on the team's list were the ones customers would pay for.
- **Segment by needs, value and WTP** — cluster customers on WTP data, not demographics. Check that segments have clear fences (features one segment wants and others don't), that salespeople can sort real clients into them, and that each segment has observable traits for marketing. Start with 3–4 segments and don't feel obliged to serve them all; size them first. The same water is free from a fountain and $5 from a minibar.
- **Leaders, fillers, killers** (index terms; details not read) — leaders drive purchase, fillers add some value, and killers put off segments and should be left out or made optional. Good/better/best tiers and bundles are built from these classifications for each segment.
- **Monetisation models** — subscription, dynamic pricing, market-based pricing (auctions), alternative-metric pricing (pay for the outcome or usage, such as Michelin charging trucking fleets per mile instead of per tyre), and freemium. Judge them on whether they are predictable, flexible, fair and transparent for customers, feasible, scalable and easy to explain. Sometimes the model itself is the innovation.
- **Warning signs** — for feature shock: R&D can't articulate a feature's customer value. For minivation: targets are easily met, channels earn maximum margins, products sell out, and deals go through without pushback on price. For hidden gems: ideas blocked by middle managers during business-model shifts. For undeads: proponents overstate appeal and there is no segmentation.
- **Price integrity** — after launch, don't cut price at the first sign of slow demand. Look first at value communication, sales execution and the segments targeted. Watch discount escalation and repricing that damages perceived quality.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- Before committing to a new feature, plan or product, run 10–20 WTP conversations with target customers using a concept, wireframe or prototype. Ask about acceptable, expensive and prohibitive prices, and ask them to rank or trade off features (most–least). Do it before the build, when people aren't in negotiating mode.
- Treat the roadmap as a WTP question. For each candidate feature, record whether target segments would pay for it, use it without paying, or be put off by it (leader, filler, killer). Build the leaders well, keep fillers cheap, and drop or isolate the killers. A team's favourite feature with no WTP signal is a feature-shock warning.
- Design plan tiers from segments, not from a feature list cut into three. Name tiers after who they serve, give each a fence feature its segment strongly wants, and give price-sensitive segments a deliberately basic tier rather than everything ("product configuration requires the guts to take away features"). Cross-check against `references/data-driven/conversion-paywalls-pricing-subscriptions.md` (3–4 tiers, honest "most popular" label).
- Choose the value metric (what you charge per: seat, project, usage, outcome) before designing the pricing page and the in-product limits. The metric shapes the UI: usage meters, limit warnings, upgrade moments, team invitations. Pick one that customers find fair and predictable and that grows with the value they get. Avoid metrics that punish the behaviour you want (charging per invite discourages collaboration).
- Look for price cliffs in the WTP answers, the points where many people move from acceptable to expensive. Set list prices just below a cliff and test charm versus round endings against brand positioning (`references/psychology/behavior-pricing-and-choice.md`).
- When a new product sells out or every deal closes without objection, treat that as evidence of underpricing (minivation), not only as success. Before raising prices on existing users, plan grandfathering and notice, because a price increase is a loss in their eyes (`references/books/thaler-misbehaving.md`).
- Write value communication as concrete benefit statements per segment ("save 3 hours a week on X"), not feature lists. Design the pricing page so that each tier's top benefit for its segment is visible before the checkmark table.
- When demand disappoints after launch, check value communication, onboarding to the "aha" moment and sales or targeting before cutting the price. Price cuts are hard to reverse and signal lower quality.
- For a disruptive or unfamiliar product, test the pricing model with customers as well as the price point (subscription vs usage vs outcome). Sometimes a new model (pay per mile, pay per result) removes the main barrier to adoption.
- Put WTP numbers in the business case. Link price, expected volume per segment, cost and cannibalisation of existing plans, and update the case as evidence comes in. A forecast without WTP data is a guess with a spreadsheet around it (the authors say about 95% of business cases are like this).
- Check each proposed segment the way Ramanujam suggests: could a salesperson or support agent sort real customers into it? Does it have a fence feature? Is it big enough to be worth a tier? If not, merge it. Three or four well-fenced tiers beat six that blur together.
- Decide early whether the launch is a skim (high price for early adopters who will pay most, lowered later) or a penetration play (low price for share and network effects). Design the onboarding, plan names and upgrade paths to match. Don't drift between the two after launch.
- In the WTP talk, separate the questions: first "is this valuable to you, and which parts?", then the three price questions, then "which of these would you drop to pay less?" Write down segment-specific reasons as well as numbers. The reasons drive copy and configuration later.
- Treat hidden gems as a portfolio risk. When the business is shifting (product to service, hardware to software, offline to online), list the ideas middle management turned down and give each one a cheap WTP test before discarding it.
- Keep behavioural tactics (anchors, a compromise middle tier, "pennies a day") honest. Use only real, purchasable tiers and real reference prices. Run them through the ethics gate in `references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`.

## Evidence & caveats
- The evidence is consulting experience: Simon-Kucher's "10,000+ projects", the Global Pricing Study, and success stories from the firm's own clients (including Porsche's Cayenne, which the authors say they worked on). Survivorship and selection bias are obvious. There is no comparison group of companies that ran WTP studies and failed anyway.
- "72% of new products fail to meet revenue or profit goals" comes from a self-reported executive survey (Simon-Kucher 2014, n = 1,615). The other failure rates quoted (65% PDMA, 75% of VC-backed startups) use different definitions. Treat them as directional, not precise.
- Stated WTP suffers from hypothetical bias: people state higher valuations in hypothetical questions than they pay in reality (Berman's guide and the WTP-methods literature it cites; context, not the authors). The three-question format is a variant of the Van Westendorp price-sensitivity meter, which has little academic validation. Use stated WTP to rank features, find cliffs and spot segments, not as a forecast. Confirm with incentive-compatible or behavioural tests where possible: discrete-choice or conjoint studies, real pre-orders, controlled price tests (`references/data-driven/experimentation-playbook.md`).
- The behavioural pricing chapter (index: Ariely, Kahneman, Thaler; anchoring, compromise effect, decoys, pennies-a-day) draws on lab findings, some of which replicate weakly in realistic settings (the decoy effect is contested; Ariely's work has credibility problems, including a retracted, fabricated field study). See `references/data-driven/conversion-paywalls-pricing-subscriptions.md` for the current state.
- The book is written from the firm's side and focuses on capturing value. "Designing around the price" can slide into charging for everything; techne's ethics gate still applies (no crippled free tiers, no hidden fees, no fake scarcity).

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/books/ulwick-jobs-to-be-done-theory-to-practice.md` says asking what customers would pay for a feature is the wrong question; ask instead what getting the job done perfectly is worth. Ramanujam asks about price directly, early and per concept. Use Ulwick's framing to choose which job and outcomes to target, and Ramanujam's structured WTP talk to price and package the solution. Both agree that price should be researched before design.
- `references/books/mom-test.md` warns that compliments and hypotheticals are worthless and that commitment (money, time, introductions) is the real signal. Ramanujam's WTP talk is hypothetical by design. Combine them: ask the price questions, then ask for a commitment (letter of intent, pre-order, paid pilot) and trust the commitment more.
- `references/books/lean-analytics-croll-yoskovitz.md` suggests "false payment" fake-door tests. Ramanujam suggests interviews and surveys. Fake doors give behavioural data but need honest disclosure; WTP talks give a range but are biased upwards. Use both, and weigh behaviour more heavily.
- `references/psychology/behavior-pricing-and-choice.md` gives the evidence levels for the psychological tactics the book's chapter 11 recommends. Where they disagree, trust that file's evidence grades.
