# Discussing Design — Adam Connor & Aaron Irizarry

Use this when: design reviews turn into lists of changes, taste fights or approval gates; you need to run a critique session, or teach a team (including non-designers and stakeholders) to give and take useful feedback; a stakeholder sends their own mockup instead of feedback, or feedback stings and you need to respond well.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Critique is a form of analysis, not approval, praise or problem-solving: it uses critical thinking to judge whether a design, as it stands, is likely to achieve the objectives the team agreed on, and why. It works only on a shared foundation (personas and scenarios as the setting; goals and principles as the finish line), with the right intent, good facilitation, and a clear rule that the designer owns the decisions afterwards. Most feedback in organizations is instead reaction (gut response) or direction (instructions), and design reviews tend to be approval gates; teams improve by naming these differences and building critique deliberately into the iteration loop.

## Core concepts
- **Three kinds of feedback:**
  - *reaction*: an emotional or instinctive response, or what people think they're expected to say;
  - *direction*: an instruction or suggestion ("make these radio buttons a dropdown"), often given without a reason;
  - *critique*: analysis tied to an objective ("if the goal is X, this element isn't effective because Y").
  Only critique tells you whether your design decisions work.
- **Critique = critical thinking against objectives** — the question is whether it's true that this design, done this way, meets these objectives. Objectives can be about utility, metrics or experience.
- **The foundation** — personas and scenarios set the "through whose eyes, in what context". Goals (a changed state caused by the product, and measurable) and principles (characteristics of the design itself, specific to this product) define success. Without them, critique depends on chance, and the discussion drifts into arguing about how to evaluate the design.
- **Design goal vs design principle** — a goal is an outcome of using the product. A principle describes a quality of the design, not how to achieve it. If designs usually pass a principle, the principle is too broad.
- **Critique vs design review** — reviews are about approval, are timed by the project schedule, end in a to-do list, have too many attendees (20–50+) and attract people pushing their own agendas. Critique is about improvement, can happen whenever the designer needs it, needs time to iterate afterwards, and works best with about six people.
- **Analysis and problem-solving don't mix** — jumping to solutions hides what the actual problem is, splits the room's attention, produces short-sighted fixes and uses up the time. Capture the idea, find the problem behind it, and move on. If a big problem appears, change the meeting's purpose explicitly.
- **Critique vs direction (Irizarry)** — direction is legitimate when work clearly breaks established patterns or style guides, or when acting on customer feedback. Good direction states the issue and the constraint ("reduce density") and leaves the designer room on how.
- **Rules of critique** — everyone is equal regardless of title; everyone is a critic, including the designer; avoid problem-solving and design decisions in the session; the designer is responsible for follow-up and decisions.
- **Facilitation toolkit** — send materials and goals in advance; open by restating goals and rules; present briefly; don't argue constraints in the room. In the session use active listening and restating, round robin, asking quiet people directly, quotas ("two strengths, one concern"), de Bono's Six Thinking Hats, the five whys, and optionally a neutral facilitator. Afterwards, share observations and open questions, follow up one-on-one, and announce the next critique.
- **When the client sends their own design** — breathe and assume good intent (they're showing rather than telling). Privately critique their version (what changed, what problem they seem to be solving, what matters to them), then critique both designs together against the agreed goals and principles, and co-create the next iteration.
- **Receiving feedback is emotional, and that's normal** — openness doesn't mean being immune to emotion. Learn your reaction pattern, pause, listen to what is actually being said (take notes or record), ask "what if they're right?", and turn it into a dialogue. Even rude feedback can contain insight: separate the content from the person afterwards.
- **Critique beyond design** — the same principles apply to managing people: documented expectations, regular structured feedback, specifics and impact rather than generalities, direct and kind delivery, close to when the event happened. Secondhand feedback is gossip.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- Before any review, write and share a one-paragraph brief: target persona and scenario, two or three measurable goals, the design principles in play, and the stage of the work. Open the session by reading it out.
- Phrase every comment as objective → element → effect → reason: "If the goal is to make people weigh their balance before buying, the balance at the bottom in body size isn't effective because it gets lost among other numbers." Reject "I don't like it" and bare instructions until they're converted to this form.
- When someone says "you should…", ask "what problem would that solve?" Log the idea, check with the room whether others see the same problem, and keep the session in analysis mode.
- Keep critiques to about six people, timed by the designer's need, with time to iterate afterwards. Keep sign-off reviews separate, and don't expect critique to happen in them.
- Use quotas ("each person: two things that work, one concern tied to a goal") and round robin, so juniors and non-designers contribute and late surprises come up in the room, not in an email weeks later.
- If a big problem dominates, say out loud "we're switching this session to explore X" rather than letting the critique quietly turn into a redesign.
- When a stakeholder mocks up their own solution, don't defend or comply. Compare both designs against the agreed goals and principles together, and pick out the concerns their version reveals.
- Give direction (not critique) only when work breaks established system patterns, accessibility rules or confirmed customer findings. State the constraint and leave the means open.
- When receiving harsh feedback, wait before replying (your known cool-down time), restate what was said, and ask the giver which goal they think is at risk. Answer in writing only once the emotional reaction has passed.
- After the session, the designer sends a short follow-up: what we learned, open questions and owners, what changes before the next critique and when it is.
- If your principles never cause a "no", rewrite them to be specific to the product (see `references/books/kholmatova-design-systems.md` on actionable principles).
- When reviewing AI-generated designs or variants, apply the same objective → element → effect test, because "looks good" is a reaction, not a critique.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a practitioner framework drawn from the authors' workshops and experience. There is no empirical study of its effects on outcomes. The benefits claimed (better collaboration, fewer surprises) are plausible but anecdotal.
- Some claims are folk psychology: "the brain can't analyze and problem solve at the same time" is a figure of speech, not an established finding. The useful point is practical: mixing modes in a group derails the session.
- Group-size advice (about six) comes from conversation dynamics as the authors have observed them, not from data. Related research on brainstorming (production blocking, conformity) supports collecting individual input before discussion. Their quotas and round robin help with that, but silent written critique first would help further.
- "Everyone is equal" is an aspiration. Hierarchy still affects candour, so facilitators often need to ask senior people to speak last.
- Some essays are a decade old and assume co-located teams. Asynchronous critique (comments in Figma, Loom walkthroughs) needs the same foundation: goals and principles written next to the work.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/process/heuristics-and-critique.md` §4 already uses this book (the objective → elements → effective? → why questions, plus reaction and direction). That's consistent, and this file adds design reviews vs critique, the foundation of goals, principles, personas and scenarios, the facilitation toolkit and the receiving side. The four-question wording there comes from the book and was not re-verified in these free essays, though "3 Kinds of Feedback" matches it.
- `references/books/articulating-design-decisions-greever.md` is about presenting and defending decisions to stakeholders. Connor and Irizarry warn against defending in critique: the designer critiques alongside everyone else. Use Greever for approval meetings and this file for improvement sessions.
- Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process and Pixar's Braintrust (in heuristics-and-critique §4.1) put more weight on the maker asking the questions and on protecting fragile early work. Use those for very early concepts, and Connor and Irizarry for work with defined goals.
- `modes/critique.md` (techne's own review mode) adds severity and impact ratings and concrete fixes, which is close to direction. When techne critiques a user's work, it should first state the goal it assumes (as the mode requires) and frame findings as objective-based critique before proposing fixes.
