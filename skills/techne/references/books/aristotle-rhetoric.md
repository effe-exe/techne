# Rhetoric — Aristotle

Use this when: you are designing any screen that asks someone to decide (signup, permission prompt, paywall, upgrade, cancellation, consent, a pitch deck or launch page); you are writing error messages, apologies or incident communications; you are judging whether a persuasive element works through the user's reasoning or around it; you are structuring an argument for stakeholders (design review, research readout).

Source: http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/rhetoric.html (Internet Classics Archive, W. Rhys Roberts translation; plain-text file `rhetoric.mb.txt`). Read in full: Book I (15 parts: definition, three kinds of oratory, goods, virtue, wrongdoing, non-technical proofs), Book II (26 parts: emotions, character types by age and fortune, common topics, example, maxim, enthymeme, spurious enthymemes, refutation) and Book III (19 parts: style, metaphor, rhythm, periods, arrangement, introduction, narration, argument, interrogation, epilogue).

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Rhetoric is the faculty of seeing, in any case, the available means of persuasion, and those means are three: the speaker's character as shown in the speech (ethos), the hearer's emotional state (pathos), and the argument itself (logos), whose core form is the enthymeme, a compact argument from what the audience already accepts. Because the audience decides, every speech must fit its hearer, its occasion and its kind (deliberating about the future, judging the past, or praising the present), and style must be clear and appropriate, natural rather than artificial, with metaphor that makes the hearer grasp something new quickly. Aristotle wants persuasion to rest on the facts and treats emotional and stylistic devices as necessary only "owing to the defects of our hearers", yet the book also catalogues tricks and spurious arguments, so it serves both as a toolkit and as a field guide to manipulation.

## Core concepts
- **Rhetoric as an art** — persuasion can be studied systematically: some people succeed by habit, and the art explains why. Its job is not to win every time but to find the best available means, as medicine does what it can for an incurable patient.
- **Know both sides, argue one** — learn to argue opposite cases not to mislead ("we must not make people believe what is wrong") but to see the facts clearly and to refute unfair arguments. True and better things are naturally easier to prove.
- **Three technical proofs** — character, emotion, argument. "Non-technical" proofs (laws, witnesses, contracts, oaths) are given, not invented; the speaker only uses them.
- **Ethos is earned in the speech** — character should come from what the speaker says, not from reputation before speaking. Its three parts: good sense (phronesis), good character (arete) and goodwill (eunoia). People give false advice from lacking one of the three.
- **The hearer sets the end** — deliberative speech (assembly) urges a future action on grounds of advantage; forensic (court) accuses or defends past acts on grounds of justice; epideictic (ceremony) praises or blames in the present on grounds of honour.
- **Enthymeme** — a rhetorical syllogism with fewer premises than formal logic, because the hearer fills in what everyone knows. It deals with probabilities and signs, not certainties. Audiences "cannot take in at a glance a complicated argument, or follow a long chain of reasoning".
- **Example** — rhetorical induction from parallel cases (historical) or invented ones (analogy, fable). Put examples after the argument as witnesses: one good one is enough; put them first and you need many.
- **Signs** — infallible signs (fever means illness) are proof; fallible signs (fast breathing means fever) can be refuted even when the fact is true.
- **Topics** — reusable lines of argument (the possible and impossible, past and future fact, greater and lesser; 28 common lines such as opposites, a fortiori, definition, consequences, inconsistency of the opponent).
- **Spurious enthymemes** — compact wording that only looks like a conclusion; puns; part-for-whole; indignant language with no proof; a single instance treated as proof; post hoc; omitting time and circumstances; "making the worse argument seem the better".
- **Emotions as analyzable** — for each emotion, know (1) the state of mind, (2) toward whom, (3) on what grounds. He covers anger/calm, friendship/enmity, fear/confidence, shame, kindness, pity, indignation, envy and emulation.
- **Anger is a response to a slight** — contempt, spite, insolence. Triggers include being forgotten (even one's name), levity when one is serious, being treated worse than everyone else, indifference to the pain caused, and slights before people whose opinion matters.
- **Calm** — people calm down toward those who admit fault and are sorry, who are serious when they are serious, who acted involuntarily, and as time passes. Denying the obvious makes it worse.
- **Fear needs hope** — fear is a picture of imminent harm; it requires some expectation of escape, because "fear sets us thinking what can be done". People who think nothing can touch them, or who feel already ruined, do not fear.
- **Confidence** — comes from having the means to cure or prevent trouble, from past successes, and from strong allies.
- **Kindness** — help to someone in need, unasked, not proclaimed afterwards, and not for the helper's benefit.
- **Character by age and fortune** — the young live by hope, trust readily and overdo everything; the old live by memory, hedge and distrust; people in their prime are moderate. Speeches that reflect the hearer's own character are well received.
- **Relative goods** — one good beats another by being an end, more lasting, more secure, rarer (or more useful because plentiful), chosen by experts, or by the majority. Things look bigger when divided into parts. What people have spent effort on seems good.
- **Style: clear and appropriate** — current words give clarity; some unfamiliarity gives distinction; artifice must be hidden because hearers who sense design suspect "some design against them". Call things by their specific names. Ambiguity is the sophist's and diviner's tool.
- **Metaphor, antithesis, actuality** — the most pleasing language helps us grasp a new idea quickly: neither obvious nor puzzling. Metaphors should come from kindred things, and words should "set the scene before our eyes", showing things in action.
- **Periods** — sentences with a visible beginning and end, short enough to take in at once; free-running prose with no stopping place is unsatisfying, as runners keep going when they can see the finish.
- **Style fits scale** — speaking to a crowd is like scene-painting, where fine detail is wasted; writing for a single reader needs the most finish.
- **Arrangement** — only two parts are necessary: state the case and prove it. An introduction exists to show the aim; skip it when the subject is short. Calls for attention belong where attention flags, not at the start. The epilogue disposes the hearer, sizes the facts, rouses feeling and recaps.
- **Narration** — neither "rapid" nor "concise" but right: just enough to make the facts plain.
- **Rebuttal order** — if the other side's argument was well received, clear it away first, because minds closed by a prior impression won't receive yours.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When designing a screen that asks for a decision (upgrade, permission, consent, cancel), write down which of the three proofs carries it: the product's credibility, the user's feeling, or the reasons and numbers. If only feeling carries it, add reasons before shipping, because Aristotle ranks the argument as the substance and emotion as an add-on needed only for distracted hearers.
- When building trust, show it inside the flow (a correct preview, an accurate estimate, a candid limitation) rather than relying on brand reputation or logos, because ethos "should be achieved by what the speaker says, not by what people think of his character before he begins".
- When auditing credibility, check all three parts of ethos: competence (good sense: no errors, accurate data), integrity (good character: no hidden terms), and goodwill (the product visibly acts in the user's interest, e.g. warns before an avoidable charge). Missing any one explains distrust even when the others are strong.
- When classifying a piece of UX writing, identify its kind: deliberative (should I do this? → talk about future advantage), forensic (what happened? → error and incident messages talk about the past accurately), epideictic (who are we? → marketing and brand). Mixing them confuses: an error message shouldn't sell, a paywall shouldn't relitigate the past.
- When writing an argument in copy, state the conclusion and the one premise the user doesn't already know; omit premises everyone knows, because the enthymeme works by letting the hearer fill in the obvious and long chains lose a lay audience.
- When using testimonials, case studies or customer logos, place them after the value argument, and use one strong, similar example rather than a wall, because examples after an argument act as witnesses and a single good one suffices; leading with examples needs many to persuade.
- When presenting metrics or claims, distinguish infallible signs from fallible ones ("used by 10,000 teams" does not prove fit for this team); never present a single instance or a correlation as proof, because Aristotle lists both as spurious enthymemes; the same discipline applies to experiment readouts (`references/data-driven/statistics-cheatsheet.md`).
- When writing an error or failure message, admit the fault plainly, say it was not intended, be serious in tone, and say what you've done to fix it, because those are the conditions that calm anger; never deny an obvious failure or joke when the user is frustrated ("levity when we are serious" is a listed trigger).
- When personalizing, get the user's name, preferences and history right or don't use them, because being forgotten is a slight that causes anger out of proportion to its size; a wrong name is worse than no name.
- When different users get different treatment (prices, features, queues), make the reason visible, because being treated worse than everyone else reads as contempt.
- When warning about a risk (security, data loss, overdue payment), make the danger concrete and near, and always pair it with a clear action the user can take, because fear without any hope of escape produces paralysis or indifference, not action; this matches modern fear-appeal research that requires efficacy messages.
- When helping users who are stuck, help unasked, at the moment of need, and don't announce your generosity afterwards ("we've saved you 3 hours!" undercuts it), because that is Aristotle's definition of kindness and what creates friendliness.
- When giving feedback on user mistakes (form validation, linting, grammar tools), don't be "too ready to show us our mistakes" or reproach the user for past errors; show the fix, because people feel friendly to those who don't nurse grievances or criticize.
- When an audience's prior is strongly against you (switching from a competitor, a skeptical stakeholder), address the strongest existing objection first, then make your case, because a mind already persuaded by the other side won't receive a new argument until room is made.
- When writing headlines, value propositions or feature names, aim for a phrase the user grasps instantly that also tells them something new, neither a platitude ("the best way to work") nor a riddle, because "the obvious and the obscure kinds convey nothing".
- When choosing a metaphor for a product concept (folders, inbox, canvas, agent), pick one from a close, familiar domain whose relations carry over, and check the mapping works both ways, because Aristotle's proportional metaphor must "apply reciprocally"; far-fetched metaphors are obscure.
- When writing benefit copy, describe the user doing the thing ("your invoices send themselves on the 1st") rather than abstract qualities ("powerful automation"), because language that sets the scene before the eyes and shows activity is most vivid.
- When writing UI text, use the specific name for things and avoid vague, hedge-everything phrasing, because ambiguity is how diviners avoid being proved wrong; vague promises ("may improve performance") are the copy equivalent.
- When structuring long content (onboarding, docs, a multi-step form), break it into units with visible ends (steps, sections, progress), each small enough to take in at once, because people keep going when they can see the finish and stumble on units that are too short or too long.
- When the surface is seen at a distance or skimmed (billboard, hero, app-store screenshot), use broad strokes and drop fine detail; when the reader is one person studying (settings, pricing details, contracts), use the most precise, finished language, because style should match the audience's distance.
- When a flow or page has an intro, cut it unless the subject is long or intricate, and put the aim first; put any "pay attention to this" cue at the point where attention drops, not at the top, because attention is highest at the start.
- When ending a flow or a pitch, recap what was done and why, make the outcome clear, and finish crisply, because Aristotle's epilogue reviews what was proved and his model ending is four short clauses.
- When a persuasive element relies on "everyone knows", claimed consensus, flattering the user's group, or indignant framing without facts, treat it as a manipulation red flag and run the ethics gate in `references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`, because these are exactly the devices Aristotle labels spurious or "nauseous excess".
- When presenting research or a design decision to stakeholders, use the two-part structure: state the recommendation, then prove it; add one example after the proof; anticipate the strongest counter-argument, because only statement and proof are essential.

## Evidence & caveats
- A 4th-century BCE treatise based on observation of Athenian courts and assemblies, not experiments. Treat everything as a lens.
- Some ideas line up with later research: source-credibility studies (Hovland and colleagues in the 1950s) found expertise and trustworthiness as dimensions of credibility, and later communication research (McCroskey and Teven, 1999) argued for goodwill as a third, close to Aristotle's three. The "fear needs hope" point resembles fear-appeal findings that threat messages work only with efficacy information. The claim that arguments should start from what the audience already accepts resembles work on motivated reasoning. These are parallels, not validations of Aristotle's text.
- Large parts are dated or offensive: rankings of men above women, approval of slavery and of evidence under torture (he does note tortured witnesses lie), and character sketches of the young and old that are stereotypes. Don't use the age profiles for personas.
- The book contains advice that is manipulative by today's standards: claim coincidences were intended, use commonplace maxims because people agree with them, flatter the audience's group, "slip in" what the judges enjoy, put insults in a third party's mouth. Aristotle presents these as available means and also as what an honest speaker must be able to detect. Do not treat "Aristotle recommends it" as ethical clearance.
- The translation (Roberts, 1924) is old; some passages in the source are fragmentary.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/masters/master-lenses.md` (Aristotle, Rhetoric) puts "brand trust, social proof that is real" under ethos. In the text, ethos is the character shown *by the speech itself*; testimonials and authority endorsements are closer to Aristotle's "witnesses" and "decisions already pronounced". The practical difference: credibility earned in the flow (accuracy, candour, goodwill) beats borrowed credibility. Keep social proof, but file it as evidence, not as the product's character.
- `references/masters/master-lenses.md` says "Aristotle himself separated rhetoric from deception". That is half right: he says the sophist differs by moral purpose and that one must not make people believe what is wrong, but the treatise teaches spurious arguments and tricks without condemning all of them. Techne's ethics gate (`SKILL.md` §3, `references/psychology/behavior-ethics-dark-patterns.md`) is stricter than Aristotle and should win.
- `references/psychology/behavior-persuasion-toolkit.md` (Cialdini, nudges) catalogues levers that mostly work through quick heuristics (peripheral route). Aristotle's preference is for argument (the enthymeme) and he treats emotional levers as concessions to weak hearers. When choosing a lever, prefer the toolkit's reason-respecting options (clear value, real comparisons) and use Aristotle's "which proof carries this?" check; trust the toolkit's evidence tags on effect sizes, since Aristotle offers none.
- `references/masters/master-lenses.md` says "pathos" covers imagery and tone. Aristotle's emotion analysis is more specific: each emotion has a state, a target and grounds. Use that triad when diagnosing an emotional reaction in research data (e.g. anger at a billing change = perceived slight + toward the company + for being treated worse than new customers).
- `modes/copy.md` and plain-language guidance favour plain words; Aristotle allows some unusual words for distinction. For functional UI copy plain language wins; Aristotle's latitude applies to brand and marketing headlines.
- `references/books/aristotle-poetics.md`: Poetics defers "thought" (proof, emotion, magnitude) to this treatise; both share the rule that style must be clear without being commonplace and that metaphor needs an eye for resemblances.
