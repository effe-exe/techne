# The Ten Books on Architecture — Vitruvius

Use this when: you need the original wording behind "firmness, commodity, delight" and the less famous principles around it; you are setting up a design system's base unit, scale and rules for breaking them; you are judging ornament, skeuomorphism or decoration that imitates structure; you are scoping or estimating a project and want a cost-overrun rule; you are deciding whether a prototype's success will survive at full scale.

Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20239 (Project Gutenberg plain text, Morgan's 1914 Harvard translation, public domain). Read: Book I in full (preface and all 7 chapters); Book II introduction and ch. 1; Book III introduction and ch. 1–4; Book IV introduction and ch. 1–2; Book V introduction and the opening of ch. 1; Book VI introduction, ch. 1–2, ch. 5 and the close of ch. 8; Book VII introduction and ch. 5; the opening of Book IX's introduction; Book X introduction, the opening of ch. 1 and the Diognetus/Callias story near the end. Not read: the technical chapters on materials, stucco, water, sundials and astronomy, and most of the machines (they have little bearing on digital product design).

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Architecture is judged by everything it touches, so the architect needs both practice and theory, and every work must be built "with due reference to durability, convenience, and beauty". Behind that triad sit six working principles (order, arrangement, eurythmy, symmetry, propriety, economy): a work is built from one standard module that every part relates to, adapted to its site, its users and its budget, and corrected for how the eye actually perceives it. Ornament should come from real structure and plausible reality, conventions should not be mixed carelessly, and the designer is accountable for cost, durability and the legal and practical problems they leave behind.

## Core concepts
- **Practice and theory** — practice without scholarship never gains authority; theory without practice "hunting the shadow, not the substance". The architect needs broad but not expert knowledge of many fields (drawing, geometry, history, philosophy, music, medicine, law, astronomy), enough to judge others' work.
- **Explain every ornament** — the architect must be able to say why each decorative element is there (the Caryatids story); decoration has a reason.
- **Honesty** — "no work can be rightly done without honesty and incorruptibility"; don't be grasping. In Book VI he says he waits to be asked rather than soliciting work.
- **Know the law** — so that disputes aren't left "for the householders to settle after the works are finished", and contracts protect both sides.
- **Six principles (Book I, ch. 2)** — *Order*: each part has due measure, and the whole is built up from a module taken from the work itself. *Arrangement*: putting things in their proper places, expressed through plan, elevation and perspective; it comes from reflection (careful thought on the effect) and invention (solving problems in new ways). *Eurythmy*: beauty and fitness in how parts are adjusted, height suited to breadth. *Symmetry*: agreement between parts and the whole in relation to one standard part. *Propriety*: fitness to the work's purpose, from prescription, usage or nature. *Economy*: managing materials and site, balancing cost and common sense, and suiting the building to its occupant.
- **The triad (Book I, ch. 3)** — durability (foundations to solid ground, materials well chosen), convenience (arrangement "presents no hindrance to use", each building correctly oriented), beauty (pleasing appearance, members in due proportion).
- **Propriety from usage** — a grand interior needs a matching entrance; don't carve Ionic dentils into a Doric cornice, because each order's usage "having been fixed long ago".
- **Propriety from nature** — orientation follows function: steady north light for galleries, east light for bedrooms and libraries.
- **Economy as substitution** — use what is available locally rather than demand rare materials; when there is no pit sand, use river or sea sand.
- **Symmetry from the body** — the human body is the model of parts proportioned to a whole (face a tenth of height, foot a sixth); units of measure (finger, palm, foot, cubit) come from the body. Columns were first sized from the ratio of a man's foot to his height.
- **Module systems** — temple designs use one module (a column's thickness) from which all intervals and heights are derived (the eustyle: intervals 2¼ modules, middle intervals 3, column height 9½).
- **Optical corrections** — the eye deceives: corner columns, seen against open air, look thinner, so make them thicker by a fiftieth; columns seen from below get proportionally thicker; a perfectly level base looks sagging, so raise its middle; columns swell slightly (entasis). "The eye is always in search of beauty."
- **Ergonomic numbers** — steps rise 9–10 inches, treads 1½–2 feet; use an odd number so the same foot starts and finishes.
- **Size to the population** — a forum should be proportionate to the number of inhabitants, neither too small to be useful nor "a desert waste for lack of population".
- **Ornament comes from structure** — Doric triglyphs began as boards covering cut-off beam ends; mutules imitate rafters; dentils imitate common rafters. So dentils under mutules are false, because "what could not happen in the original would have no valid reason for existence in the copy".
- **Representations must be possible** — Vitruvius condemns wall paintings of reeds holding up roofs and figures sprouting from stalks: "Such things do not exist and cannot exist and never have existed." The mathematician Licymnius made a painter remove a stage set showing buildings on top of a tiled roof.
- **Expensive colour replaces craft** — painters now cover walls in costly vermilion and blue that look brilliant "even although they are inartistically applied", and the money spent stops people noticing what's missing.
- **Adapt the standard to the site** — first fix the standard of symmetry, then add or subtract to suit site, use and appearance, "in such fashion that the buildings lose nothing thereby".
- **Public and private rooms** — some rooms nobody may enter without invitation; others anyone may. Houses are planned to suit the occupant's actual social and business needs.
- **Dinocrates' Mount Athos** — a magnificent design for a city held in a giant statue's hand, rejected by Alexander because no fields could feed it. Excellent composition, bad site.
- **Ephesus cost law** — the architect pledges his property against his estimate: within estimate, honours; up to 25% over, the treasury pays; beyond 25%, the architect pays. Owners facing a 50% overrun "lose hope" and abandon what they've spent.
- **Models that fail at scale** — Callias won the Rhodians over with a model crane that would capture siege towers; when a real 135-foot siege tower came, he admitted it was impossible. Some things work as models and at scale, some can't be modelled, and some "appear feasible in models" but fail when enlarged.
- **Laymen can judge, architects must foresee** — anyone can recognize good work once it's finished; the architect has to see its beauty, convenience and propriety before it exists, and should take advice from workmen and laymen.
- **Write briefly for busy readers** — technical vocabulary is obscure, so explain it in a few clear sentences and keep books short, because readers' "intervals of leisure are small".
- **Credit sources; don't plagiarize** — Vitruvius praises those who wrote down their knowledge, condemns those who publish others' work as their own (Aristophanes exposing plagiarist poets), and openly names the predecessors he draws from.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When reviewing any feature, check the full triad, not just one leg: durability (performance, error and offline states, accessibility, no regressions), convenience (the task flows with no hindrance) and beauty (proportion, finish), because the triad is the minimum, and the six principles behind it say how to get there.
- When setting up a design system, pick one base unit (e.g. 4 or 8px; a type size; a column width) and derive every spacing, size and radius from it as named multiples, because Vitruvius' symmetry is "relation between the different parts and the whole general scheme, in accordance with a certain part selected as standard"; see `references/build/tokens-bridge.md`.
- When the system's math looks wrong to the eye (a circle icon looking smaller than a square, text looking off-centre in a button, thin strokes vanishing in dark mode), make deliberate optical corrections and document them as named exceptions, because Vitruvius thickens corner columns by 1/50 and raises the stylobate's centre "to counteract the ocular deception". Fix the standard first, then vary from it on purpose.
- When an element is seen in different contexts (small vs large, dense vs open layout, light vs dark), adjust weight and size for each, as columns get thicker as spacing widens, because the same value looks "thin and mean" in open space and "swollen" in tight space.
- When adding decoration that imitates a physical object (shadows, textures, stitched leather, depth effects), check that it shows something that could plausibly exist and that it corresponds to real structure (e.g. elevation means the layer is actually above), because ornament grew from structure, and "what could not happen in the original" has no reason in the copy.
- When combining patterns from different design systems or platforms, don't mix their idioms in one component (iOS sheets with Material FABs, two different elevation systems), because carving one order's ornaments into another's cornice spoils the effect of conventions "fixed long ago".
- When the entry to an experience doesn't match its depth (a polished app behind a crude signup, or a lavish landing page for a thin product), bring them into proportion, because a grand interior needs a matching entrance and vice versa.
- When a community, marketplace or social feature launches, size its visible space to the actual population (fewer categories, a combined feed, seeded content), because a forum too large for its users looks like "a desert waste".
- When choosing visual polish under budget pressure, prefer proportion and craft over expensive effects (3D renders, custom illustration, heavy motion) that dazzle without fixing composition, because brilliant colour "inartistically applied" stops people noticing the missing craft.
- When estimating a design or build project, commit to an estimate and treat more than 25% over as a planning failure to review, not a routine slip, because the Ephesus law draws the line there and owners abandon projects that overrun by around 50%.
- When a prototype or demo wins approval, ask whether its success depends on scale-free principles or on the small model (hand-picked data, a few users, no latency, no edge cases), and test the risky part at realistic scale before committing, because Callias' model "appeared feasible" and failed at full size.
- When a concept is brilliant but its context can't sustain it (no data to feed it, no business model, no distribution), set it aside the way Alexander did with Dinocrates, and keep the designer, because composition is not viability.
- When a choice depends on context (light, density, device, locale), design for the actual setting first, as houses vary with climate and rooms with orientation; a dashboard used in a bright warehouse is a different design from one used in an office.
- When designing spaces of a product, separate "public rooms" (shared, visible to collaborators or the public) from "private rooms" (entered only by invitation) and make the boundary obvious, because Vitruvius plans houses around who may enter where without invitation.
- When choosing materials (libraries, fonts, components, vendors), use what is available and sound rather than insisting on something rare and costly, because economy means not demanding things "which cannot be found or made ready without great expense".
- When adding any decorative element, be able to explain in one sentence why it is there; if you can't, cut it, because the architect must explain the idea behind each ornament to "inquirers".
- When handing off, settle the edge cases, legal and policy points (data retention, permissions, accessibility conformance) before build, because the architect should not leave "disputed points for the householders to settle after the works are finished".
- When presenting unbuilt work to stakeholders, show it as close to finished as practical (realistic prototype, real data), and gather input from implementers and users, because laymen judge well only when they can see the finished thing.
- When writing documentation for a design system, explain each technical term in a sentence, keep sections short and self-contained, and put each topic in one place, because Vitruvius wrote short books so busy readers find a topic "in one complete treatment".
- When building on others' work (patterns, research, open-source), credit the sources openly, because Vitruvius distinguishes drawing from predecessors "as it were water from springs" from publishing others' work as one's own.

## Evidence & caveats
- A Roman architect's handbook from about 30–15 BCE, mixing practical rules, conventions and anecdote. It is not empirical; treat everything as a lens. Translation by Morgan (1914).
- Many claims are factually wrong by modern knowledge: four-element physiology, examining sheep livers to test a site, climate determining intelligence and courage. Book VI ch. 1 contains ethnic and climatic determinism ending with Romans as the perfect people. Ignore all of this.
- Gendered readings (Doric = male, Ionic = matron, Corinthian = maiden) and the stories of the Caryatids as enslaved women are of their time.
- The body proportions are simple integer fractions (1/10, 1/8, 1/6), not the golden ratio. The "Vitruvian man" fitting circle and square is Vitruvius' claim, later drawn by Leonardo; it is an ideal, not anthropometry.
- Optical corrections in Greek temples (entasis, curved stylobates) are real and documented in surviving buildings; whether Vitruvius' explanation (the eye's deception) is why they were made is debated.
- The specific step numbers are Roman inches and feet, roughly 22–25 cm rise and 45–60 cm tread; modern building codes use different ranges.
- Self-interest: Vitruvius writes to Augustus for patronage and complains about less educated but better-connected rivals; some of his professional advice is partly self-promotion.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/masters/master-lenses.md` (Vitruvius) translates the triad as solid, useful, delightful and says "any one missing is failure". The text (Morgan) is "durability, convenience, and beauty" for public buildings, and Book I ch. 2 gives six further principles (order, arrangement, eurythmy, symmetry, propriety, economy) that are more operational for digital work. The "any one missing is failure" reading is an interpretation; Vitruvius doesn't say it, though it fits.
- `references/masters/master-lenses.md` puts accessibility and state coverage under firmitas. Vitruvius puts usability under convenience ("no hindrance to use") and fitness to occupant under propriety and economy; accessibility fits there at least as well. Either mapping is acceptable; state which one you use.
- `SKILL.md` §7 lists the golden ratio as a UI myth. Vitruvius, often cited for it, gives integer fractions and modular multiples, so he supports "build from one module" and not "use φ".
- `references/books/aristotle-poetics.md` ranks spectacle last; Vitruvius values beauty as one of three co-equal requirements and spends pages on optical refinement. For functional surfaces, follow the convergence: structure first, then refine appearance deliberately.
- `references/books/taste-for-makers.md` (Graham: "good design resembles nature") agrees with Vitruvius' body-based proportions and tree-tapering columns. Both are analogies; neither is evidence.
- `references/inspiration/inspiration-method.md` §8 (transform, don't copy) matches Vitruvius on crediting sources; he is harsher on plagiarism and silent on remixing. `references/books/everything-is-a-remix.md` (if present) argues more openly for copying as the basis of creation; the two agree that attribution is the dividing line.
