# Creativity, Inc. — Ed Catmull (with Amy Wallace)

Use this when: setting up design critiques or review forums that need candour without crushing early work; deciding how to judge a messy early concept (or the person leading it); running postmortems after a launch, especially a successful one; protecting exploration from delivery pressure.

Basis note: the book is not free, and the official excerpts (Fast Company, HBR 2008 article) were blocked or behind a paywall, so they were not used. These notes rest on Catmull's own words in a long recorded interview with transcript (Jason Aten, *Creative Effort*, read in full) and Stanford GSB's report of his 2007 talk "Keep Your Crises Small" with direct quotes. That covers the Braintrust, "ugly babies", the director-first development process, the "hungry beast", and seeking out problems. Not covered: the book's chapters on fear and failure in depth, "the Hidden", randomness, the Disney Animation turnaround in detail, the "Notes Day" exercise, mental models of leadership, and the Steve Jobs afterword beyond the interview. Coverage is low.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Original work is ugly at the start, and a creative organisation's job is to protect it long enough for a team to fix it, using candid peer feedback that carries no authority. Leaders should bet on people (a director the team believes in) rather than judge raw ideas, because the final product usually differs radically from the pitch. Success hides problems, so leaders must keep looking for small crises, run honest postmortems and assume about half of their own ideas will not work.

## Core concepts
- **Ugly babies** — truly original ideas fit no existing norm, so early versions are broken; "all our movies suck" at first. You cannot judge quality at the start, only whether the team wants it and believes it can solve the problems.
- **The hungry beast** — production's constant need for work. Left in charge, it forces immature ideas forward to keep people busy; the ugly baby needs protection from it.
- **Bet on the director, not the idea** — pick someone who can lead a story team; give them a small team and about a year to develop three ideas; choose the one the director really wants to make, since the final film usually bears little resemblance to the pitch.
- **Three ideas to get unstuck** — asking for three concepts is less about choice than about giving people somewhere to go when they hit a wall.
- **Failure signal = loss of team belief** — the only sign to change course is when the team no longer believes the leader can solve the problem; then change the leader, not necessarily the premise (Ratatouille kept its premise under a new director).
- **The Braintrust** — a peer group of experienced makers who screen work in progress and give candid notes. Rules: the group has no authority and cannot tell the team what to do; it names what doesn't work, the team finds the fix; the most powerful people stay quiet for the first 10–15 minutes so they don't set the tone; the person who can't avoid shifting the room (Steve Jobs) is kept out.
- **Problems, not failures** — in the room the language is "this doesn't work", so the idea is judged, not the person. Catmull now thinks his book's talk of failure was slightly misleading for that reason.
- **An outside force helps** — someone with no stake in the story but a stake in your success sees what insiders stop hearing; a group can learn to not hear each other until an outsider repeats the same note.
- **Fresh eyes and a live audience** — screenings always include people who have not seen the work, because an audience changes how you perceive it.
- **Adapt group size** — larger sessions when things go well (to train people in the mechanism), small ones for knotty problems, because big rooms invite performing.
- **Seek out small crises** — organisations are inherently unstable; dissect hits as well as misses, and fix small problems before they compound (Evans & Sutherland and SGI as warnings).
- **People over ideas** — a good team will fix a mediocre idea; a mediocre team will ruin a good one.
- **Half my ideas are a crock** — expect to be wrong often and build the habit of noticing and fixing quickly instead of defending.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When reviewing an early concept, judge the problem framing, the team's conviction and whether the issues seem solvable, not its polish or how it compares to finished products, because originality is invisible in first drafts.
- When running a design critique, state up front that reviewers name problems and the owner decides the fix, because removing authority is what lets critique be candid without becoming a mandate.
- When the most senior person attends critique, have them speak last (or after the first 10–15 minutes), because the first strong opinion anchors the room.
- When a reviewer's presence alone changes the discussion (founder, executive), review with them separately rather than in the working session.
- When phrasing feedback, say "this part doesn't work for me because…" rather than proposing the solution, because the visible symptom often has its cause earlier in the flow, and the team needs time to find it.
- When a project stalls, invite a trusted outsider from another team who wants you to succeed, because insiders tune out notes they have already dismissed.
- When delivery pressure demands output from an exploration, separate the exploratory team from the production schedule, because "feeding the beast" pushes unready concepts into build.
- When asking a designer for directions, request three distinct concepts, mainly so they can switch when stuck, and expect the chosen one to change a lot.
- When deciding whether to restart or kill a project, check whether the team still believes in the lead, not whether the current version is good.
- When a launch succeeds, still hold a postmortem and look for the small problems success is hiding, because unexamined wins breed the next crisis.
- When testing work in progress, always include people who have not seen it before, because familiarity blinds the team.
- When a critique keeps returning the same note that the team keeps dismissing, have someone neutral restate it, because groups learn to stop hearing each other.
- When critique sessions grow large and people start performing, split off a small working group for the hard problem and keep the big session for teaching the format.
- When a team is two studios or two product lines, let them review each other's work occasionally but keep them independent, because peers from outside the project bring objectivity without the threat of a mandate.

## How to run a Braintrust-style design review (synthesis)
1. Show the actual work (prototype, flow, build), not slides about it.
2. Owner states the goal and what feels broken; reviewers watch with fresh eyes.
3. Senior people hold back for the first 10–15 minutes.
4. Notes describe problems and their effect on the user, not prescribed fixes.
5. The owner leaves with notes and full authority over what to change.
6. Follow up later (days, not minutes) once the team has had time to find solutions.

## Evidence & caveats
- This is a memoir and management philosophy from one exceptional company: anecdote, survivorship and hindsight, not data. Pixar's record (21 of 22 started films completed in Catmull's time, by his count) cannot separate process from talent, money and luck.
- The Braintrust is well-documented as a practice but has no controlled evidence. Related research supports parts of it: anchoring by high-status speakers, and the value of psychological safety for team learning (Edmondson's work), but techne has not verified those links here.
- Catmull himself says the original group's candour was partly luck and partly a technical culture of problem-solving; transplanting the ritual without the trust may fail.
- Film development (years, a single creative lead, no live metrics) differs from product design, where shipping and measuring is cheap. Use the critique mechanics; don't use "protect the ugly baby" to avoid testing with users.
- Interview statements are Catmull's recollections years later and may be smoothed.

## Disagreements with other references
- `creative-selection-kocienda.md` describes Apple's demo culture with a single decisive executive (Jobs) picking among demos; Pixar deliberately removed authority from review. Use Pixar's model for peer critique, Apple's for a clear final decision-maker; both rely on concrete work shown early and often.
- `process/heuristics-and-critique.md` already lists the Braintrust as [weak/anecdotal but influential] and recommends Connor & Irizarry's objective-based critique (`connor-irizarry-discussing-design.md`). Combine: Braintrust rules for who speaks and who decides, Discussing Design for how each comment is framed.
- techne's evidence-first stance (`SKILL.md` §2) says taste proposes and data decides. Catmull's "you can't judge it early" fits that: protect early exploration, then let users and metrics judge, rather than letting reviewers' taste kill or crown ideas.
- `shape-up.md` bets on scoped pitches with fixed appetite; Pixar bets on people with open-ended time. Product teams usually need Shape Up's time box plus Pixar's protection from production pressure.
