# Visual Intelligence: How We Create What We See — Donald D. Hoffman

Use this when: designing icons, logos, silhouettes or illustrations that must be recognized fast and at small sizes; judging whether a shape will be read the intended way when figure and ground could flip (negative-space logos, cut-out cards, masks); deciding where a draggable handle, tab or protrusion will read as a separate "part"; explaining why users see edges, surfaces or motion that aren't drawn; arguing that an interface should be a useful fiction rather than a faithful picture of the system underneath.

Basis: the book (Norton, 1998; paperback 2000) is not free. These notes rest on Hoffman's own free material on his UC Irvine pages: the book's official companion pages (the full Preface, table of contents, and the chapter 6 motion-demonstration index, plus his illusions page listing the book's illustrations); "Parts of Recognition" (Hoffman & Richards, Cognition 1984), which the book popularizes as the parts and figure-ground material, read in full; "Salience of Visual Parts" (Hoffman & Singh, Cognition 1997), read for its theory, method and results; "Parsing Silhouettes: The Short-Cut Rule" (Singh, Seyranian & Hoffman, 1999 preprint), read for its theory; "The Construction of Visual Reality" (Hoffman, book chapter, c. 2012), which restates the book's central "vision is construction" thesis and its last chapter's interface idea, read in full; and "Perception and Evolution" (Bennett, Hoffman & Prakash, 2000 draft chapter), skimmed. The book's chapters on 3D from line drawings (ch. 2), subjective surfaces (ch. 3), colour (ch. 5), motion (ch. 6) and touch (ch. 7) are **not** read; the list of about 35 "rules of vision" the book is known for is **not** verified here.

## The big idea (3 sentences)
Vision isn't a camera; it is a fast, mostly unconscious construction of shapes, depths, colours and motions from about 120 million photoreceptor counts per eye, and any image is compatible with infinitely many worlds. To pick one, vision follows rules that exploit regularities of nature: for example, it cuts shapes into parts at deep concavities, because that is where two objects stuck together meet. In Hoffman's later framing (the book's last chapter), what we construct is not a true picture of the world but a species-specific *interface*, like a desktop whose icons guide useful action without resembling the hardware behind them.

## Core concepts
- **Vision as construction** — about half the cortex works on vision; illusions are cases where we catch the construction in the act. Neon colour spreading is a standard example: cover the black lines and the "glowing" surface disappears.
- **Inference licensed by regularities** — because an image never logically fixes the world, every percept is an inductive bet. It is strong only when a regularity makes the "deception probability" (image looks like X but the world is not X) near zero. Explaining a perception means naming the premises, the conclusion and the regularity that links them.
- **Transversality** — when two surfaces interpenetrate they almost always meet in a crease of concave discontinuity (a straw in a drink, a candle in a cake). Vision uses this to find parts without knowing what the parts look like.
- **Minima rule** — divide a surface into parts at negative minima of each principal curvature; in 2D silhouettes, divide the outline at negative minima of curvature (deepest points of concavities, judged from the figure side). Boundary-based, so it works for any shape, unlike part catalogues (cylinders, cones, geons).
- **Figure-ground changes the parts** — what is concave from one side is convex from the other, so when figure and ground flip, the part boundaries move. This explains the Schröder staircase (dots on one step land on two), Attneave's scribbled circle, and the face–vase figure (forehead, nose, lips, chin vs base, stem, bowl, lip). Ambiguities that don't flip figure and ground (duck–rabbit) keep the same parts.
- **Parts predict similarity** — in a quick look, a shape with the same part boundaries looks more similar than one with an identical contour but reversed figure and ground.
- **Part salience** (1997) — a part stands out more when (a) it is large relative to the whole, (b) it protrudes more (more perimeter outside the cut), and (c) its boundaries are sharper (stronger turning at the concavity). Salient parts bias which side is seen as figure: in their face–vase tests, "vase" reports ranged from 25% to 74% across three versions of the same figure that differed only in part salience (10 subjects).
- **Short-cut rule** (1999) — vision prefers the shortest cut that joins boundary points to separate a part, and a cut can create part boundaries of its own.
- **Interface theory** — evolutionary game simulations (Mark, Marion & Hoffman 2010) are said to show that perceptions tuned to fitness payoffs beat perceptions tuned to truth. Perception is a quick, cheap guide to adaptive behaviour: take it seriously (don't step in front of the bus) but not literally. Dragonflies laying eggs on oil and asphalt, which polarize light more than water, illustrate cheap perceptual tricks failing in new environments.
- **Illusion, redefined** — an illusion is a perception most people have in a given context that is not an adaptive guide to behaviour, not simply a "false" one.
- **Audience** — the Preface names marketers, advertisers, graphic designers and VR builders as readers: images must pass the viewer's visual intelligence before they reach reason or emotion.

## Actionable heuristics for product design
- When designing an icon, logo or illustration that must read at small size, put its meaningful divisions at deep concavities of the silhouette, because vision segments outlines at negative curvature minima. Parts that are separated only by colour or inner lines, with a smooth outer contour, will be read as one blob when shrunk or shown as a monochrome glyph.
- When an icon set must stay distinguishable, make the silhouettes differ in their part structure (number, size and placement of protrusions), not just in inner detail, because part structure is what drives quick shape recognition and similarity.
- When a design uses negative space (a logo with a hidden shape, a card with a cut-out, a mask, a toggle knob in a track), decide which side must be figure and reinforce it with fill, contrast, convexity and part salience. If figure and ground can flip, the parts users see will flip too, and the reading can change.
- When you want a handle, tab, notch, badge or drag affordance to read as a separate graspable part, make it protrude, give it a sharp concave junction with the body, and don't make it tiny relative to the whole, because those three factors raise part salience. Soft, blended junctions make it look like part of the body.
- When a layout relies on implied edges (borderless cards on tinted backgrounds, aligned whitespace, illusory contours in logos), test it at small sizes, in greyscale and at low contrast, because users' visual systems construct those surfaces and the construction can fail or produce shapes you didn't intend (neon-like spreading, phantom lines).
- When designing an interface metaphor, aim for useful action rather than a faithful picture of the system. A file icon doesn't look like the file; it only has to make the right actions obvious and the dangerous ones (trash, delete, send) unmistakable. Make the metaphor consistent so users can take it seriously, and make irreversible actions look irreversible.
- When a perceptual shortcut users rely on breaks in a new context (a platform convention, a familiar colour code, a position), expect "ecological trap" errors. Users will trust the cue over the content, so a destructive action placed where a safe one usually sits will be hit.
- When critiquing a visual, name the rule the viewer's eye is likely to apply (concavity cuts, figure from convexity and salience, shortest cut) and predict the reading, then check it with a 5-second or first-impression test rather than trusting the designer's own eye, which already knows the intended reading.

## Evidence & caveats
- The minima rule and part-salience findings are peer-reviewed psychophysics and widely cited in shape perception; part boundaries at concavities have held up in later work by others. But the experiments read here are small (about 10 subjects per experiment in the 1997 salience studies) and use simple line drawings, so effect sizes don't transfer directly to real icons or UI. Other theories (skeleton/medial-axis representations, geons) also predict parts; treat the minima rule as a strong default, not a law [moderate].
- "Vision is construction" is mainstream in vision science; the book's specific list of rules is a popular synthesis I did not read and can't vouch for rule by rule.
- The interface theory is Hoffman's later, contested philosophical position. The evolutionary simulations depend on modelling choices, and critics argue they rely on a narrow meaning of "veridical". Use it as a design metaphor, not as established science [weak as a claim about reality].
- The book is opinionated popular science (1998) and predates most work on screens, touch and modern icon systems. No product-design data is in the material read here.
- The minima rule is a theory of *what* is computed, not an algorithm; the authors note that curvature minima are hard to measure from noisy images and that part boundaries depend on the scale at which a shape is viewed (a nose at one scale, a wart on it at another). For icons, that means part structure must survive the smallest size you ship.
- The Preface's claims about computer vision ("can't yet buy a vision machine that beats a toddler") are dated by modern deep learning.

## Disagreements with other references
- `references/books/gibson-ecological-approach.md` argues the opposite of the book's core: perception is *direct* pick-up of information in the light, not construction or inference. For design both lead to similar advice (make structure visible, rely on what users perceive without thought), but they disagree on mechanism. Use Gibson for motion, occlusion and spatial layout; use Hoffman for static shape, silhouettes and figure-ground in icons and illustrations.
- `references/books/wagemans-gestalt-century.md` and `references/books/wertheimer-gestalt-laws.md` cover grouping and figure-ground. Hoffman's parts rules add a geometric account of *where* a shape splits; they don't replace the grouping laws for layout.
- `references/books/design-of-everyday-things.md` uses "affordances" and "signifiers" for what actions look possible. Hoffman's interface view supports the signifier idea (icons need not resemble anything, only guide action) but doesn't address learned conventions; trust Norman and platform guidelines on conventions.
